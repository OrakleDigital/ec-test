Introduction

Malignant Hypertension in a 31-Year-Old Woman

History of Present Illness

Welcome. Your patient today is Laura W.

Laura W. is a 31-year-old woman, gravida 3 para 2, with two previous full-term pregnancies and one spontaneous abortion. In the third trimester of her last pregnancy 2 years ago, she developed hypertension, managed with a single antihypertensive, labetalol (a beta-blocker), which she discontinued 3 months after delivery without medical consultation.

She presented to her primary care physician 7 days ago with a persistent headache, which had been ongoing for weeks, nausea, vomiting, and general malaise. She was referred to the emergency room on the same day. She denied using recreational drugs, such as amphetamines or cocaine. Her blood pressure (BP) was recorded at 230/140 mmHg during the visit. The physician administered captopril to reduce her BP and referred her to the emergency room. Upon hospital admission, her BP remained high at 210/130 mmHg, and blood tests revealed acute kidney failure with a serum creatinine level of 3 mg/dL and an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of 11 mL/min/1.73 m2.

For BP control, Laura was started on intravenous nitroprusside and angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors. Despite the stabilization of her BP the following day, her renal function continued to deteriorate, with serum creatinine rising to 4.5 mg/dL. While her clinical condition has since stabilized, the underlying etiology of her progressive kidney impairment remains a significant concern.

Case Review

Laura is a 31-year-old woman with a history of hypertension who presented with severe malignant hypertension (230/140 mmHg), persistent headaches, and acute kidney injury (serum creatinine 4.5 mg/dL, eGFR 11 mL/min/1.73m²). Despite aggressive blood pressure control, her renal function continued to decline, prompting further evaluation. Laboratory findings revealed microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and no severe ADAMTS13 deficiency, consistent with atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome presenting with malignant hypertension. Recent evidence indicates that malignant hypertension in this context is a consequence of complement-mediated TMA rather than a trigger, and the presence of TMA in patients with malignant hypertension should guide the diagnosis toward primary aHUS .

Given the progressive renal dysfunction and ongoing thrombotic microangiopathy, complement inhibition was initiated. The faculty author of today’s case opted to use Ravulizumab in this patient. Given the need for sustained complement inhibition to prevent further microvascular damage, Ravulizumab was selected as a practical long-term treatment approach.

Thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA)

Thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) is characterized by microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and organ injury due to endothelial damage and vascular occlusion. While primary forms like thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) and hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS) are well-known, TMA more often occurs secondary to conditions such as infections, pregnancy, autoimmune diseases, or malignant hypertension. .

In TMA, laboratory findings typically reveal thrombocytopenia, anemia, and schistocytes on a blood smear, elevated LDH, low haptoglobin, reticulocytosis, and unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia, indicating hemolysis. The direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test is negative, and coagulation tests are typically normal unless severe DIC is present .

CLASSIFICATION OF TMAs

Major categories of TMAs include thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) and hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS), with further subclassifications such as typical HUS, atypical HUS (aHUS), and aHUS with triggers. Triggered by conditions like infections, pregnancy, malignancy, or transplantation .

TTP is primarily caused by a severe deficiency in ADAMTS13 activity (5-10% activity depending on the assay , resulting from either autoantibodies (acquired TTP) or genetic mutations (Upshaw-Shulman syndrome). It is a systemic disorder affecting multiple organs, including the central nervous system, heart, and kidneys. HUS, by contrast, primarily affects the kidneys and is divided into STEC-HUS (caused by Shiga toxin-producing E. coli) and aHUS (associated with complement dysregulation) .

STEC-HUS commonly occurs in children following bloody diarrhea caused by E. coli , while aHUS has a more insidious onset. aHUS can be further classified into primary aHUS, driven by genetic mutations in complement regulatory proteins, and trigger aHUS, triggered by conditions like cancer, infections, pregnancy, drugs, or transplantation . Despite differences in pathogenesis, Hemolytic Uremic syndromes (HUS) subtypes share clinical features, including hemolysis, thrombocytopenia, and kidney failure, with complement dysregulation playing a central role in primary aHUS .

Atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (aHUS)

Atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (aHUS) is a rare subtype of thrombotic microangiopathy characterized by microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and kidney dysfunction. This condition is associated with significant morbidity and mortality and often leads to poor clinical outcomes. While primarily affecting the kidneys, aHUS can also result in dysfunction across multiple organ systems. The disorder arises from genetic abnormalities in the complement alternative pathway, causing excessive complement system activation and the development of microvascular thrombi. These abnormalities may involve mutations in key complement regulatory genes or autoantibodies targeting specific complement factors .

mHTN

Malignant hypertension (mHTN) may not simply trigger aHUS but can itself be a manifestation of underlying complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), such as aHUS. Recent evidence suggests that in many cases previously attributed to pressure-induced endothelial injury, the hypertensive crisis may actually result from ongoing microvascular damage driven by dysregulated complement activation .

In this model, hypertension is a downstream symptom of systemic TMA, not the initiating cause. Recognition of this pathophysiology is critical, as missed or delayed diagnosis of aHUS can lead to rapid progression to irreversible renal injury .

Blood pressure control is essential for mHTN-induced TMA , while complement inhibitors may benefit patients with underlying complement abnormalities .

Differential Diagnosis

Differentiating cases of MHT from aHUS is challenging because 30% to 50% of patients with a clinical diagnosis of aHUS do not have an identified pathogenic variant or autoantibody to explain complement dysregulation .

According to Cavero et al. (2023), several clinical features can help differentiate aHUS from hypertensive emergency in patients presenting with malignant hypertension. Patients with aHUS were generally younger, more often female, and commonly lacked traditional risk factors for chronic hypertension, such as obesity, family history, or dietary and lifestyle contributors. While both groups may present with elevated blood pressure, those with aHUS often had more severe kidney dysfunction relative to the degree of hypertension, and a much higher likelihood of requiring dialysis. Hematologic findings were also key: microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and elevated LDH were common in aHUS but largely absent in primary hypertension. These distinguishing features highlight the need to consider aHUS when severe renal and hematologic abnormalities are present, even if the patient does not have a history of longstanding hypertension.

The Value of Complement Studies in TMA

Complement studies play a crucial role in diagnosing and managing thrombotic microangiopathies (TMA), particularly atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome, a variant characterized by complement dysregulation . Genetic mutations in complement regulatory proteins, including factor H (FH), factor I (FI), and membrane cofactor protein (MCP or CD46), are identified in 50% to 60% of aHUS cases . However, lack of identifiable mutations in complement proteins does not rule out the possibility of aHUS, as complement dysregulation may result from other mechanisms such as autoantibodies .

These mutations disrupt the regulation of the alternative complement pathway, leading to excessive complement activation and the formation of microvascular thrombi. Mutations in complement proteins C3 and factor B (FB) are less common but contribute to hyperactivation of the complement cascade through reduced regulation or increased convertase stability, respectively. Complement studies provide insight into the molecular pathogenesis of TMA and guide targeted therapies like eculizumab or ravulizumab, which inhibit complement activation .

Testing for specific complement abnormalities has diagnostic and prognostic value in TMA. FH mutations, the most common in aHUS, impair complement regulation on cell surfaces, particularly in the renal microvasculature, resulting in severe kidney damage . FI and FB mutations, while less frequent, are associated with distinct clinical outcomes, such as frequent relapses and varying risks of progression to end-stage renal disease .

The Role of Complement Inhibitors

Complement inhibitors have revolutionized the treatment of aHUS. Without treatment, aHUS carries a poor prognosis, with high rates of end-stage renal disease and death. A French registry study reported that 17% of pediatric and 46% of adult patients progressed to ESRD or death within one month of diagnosis, increasing to 29% and 56% after one year .

Eculizumab, the first approved complement inhibitor for aHUS, targets complement protein C5 to block terminal complement activation. It has shown significant efficacy in improving renal and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) outcomes, but its administration requires infusions every 14 ± 2 days, This frequent dosing schedule poses challenges, including treatment burden and infusion-related adverse effects, making long-term adherence more difficult .

Ravulizumab, an anti-C5 monoclonal antibody with more sustained efficacy, was developed to address these issues. Prompt initiation of complement inhibitors, such as ravulizumab, is essential for achieving complete TMA response and hematologic normalization in the majority of patients, as demonstrated in pivotal trials . With dosing intervals extended to several weeks (every 4 or 8 weeks depending on body weight) and a newly approved 100 mg/mL formulation that reduces infusion times, ravulizumab minimizes the treatment burden while maintaining efficacy and safety . While haematological parameters improve rapidly with C5 inhibition, improvement in renal outcomes is time-dependent – in some patients, renal function continues to improve for up to 2 years after treatment initiation .

Treatment Duration

Guidelines state that treatment should be continued for a minimum of 6 months, beyond which the length of treatment needs to be considered for each patient individually. After discontinuing treatment with ravulizumab, patients should be monitored for clinical symptoms and laboratory signs of TMA complications for at least 12 months .

Patients with DGKE mutations are unlikely to benefit from ravulizumab or other complement C5 inhibitors because the complement pathway is intact in these conditions.

As a result, treatment with C5 inhibitors is typically discontinued in these patients if no response is observed .

In conclusion, Atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (aHUS) is a rare but severe thrombotic microangiopathy driven by complement dysregulation, often associated with genetic mutations in complement regulatory proteins or complement-activating autoantibodies. Without treatment, aHUS carries a high risk of end-stage renal disease, death, and systemic complications affecting multiple organ systems. The advent of complement inhibitors, such as eculizumab and ravulizumab, has transformed aHUS management, significantly improving renal and systemic outcomes while reducing treatment burden.
