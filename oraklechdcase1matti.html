Introduction

History of Present Illness

Welcome. Your patient today is Matti Z.

A 39-year-old mechanic with diabetes reported a 12-year history of redness, scaling, and painful fissuring of the hands. He had intermittently applied various potent topical corticosteroids, which produced only transient improvement, with symptoms reliably recurring on cessation. He is the sole income earner for his family, caring for a disabled spouse, and although phototherapy had been prescribed previously, he was unable to attend the required regular sessions. He reports frequent occupational exposure to oils, solvents, detergents, and repeated mechanical friction and wet work during vehicle repair, with worsening after prolonged work shifts and repeated hand washing.

Given the persistent course despite prolonged topical steroid use, he was referred for dermatological evaluation.

Case Review

Key Clinical Takeaways

Disease Burden

  • CHD significantly impairs function, productivity, and quality of life, often contributing to work disability and compensation claims.
  • Under-treatment is common, and symptoms frequently recur despite standard therapies.

Diagnosis & Evaluation

  • CHD is common, chronic, and burdensome, often affecting work and quality of life.
  • Diagnosis requires differentiating eczema from psoriasis, tinea manuum, allergic/irritant contact dermatitis, and rarer entities such as cutaneous T-cell lymphoma or Bowen’s disease.
  • History should include atopy, childhood eczema, occupational exposures, recent irritants, and hand-washing frequency.
  • Patch testing is essential when contact allergy is suspected or when dermatitis worsens despite therapy.
  • Skin scrapings and cultures help exclude fungal infection .

First-Line & Conventional Treatment

  • Potent or super-potent topical corticosteroids are first-line but often yield only temporary improvement.
  • Emollients, avoidance of irritants/allergens, and patient education remain foundational .

Phototherapy

  • Narrowband UVB and PUVA can be effective, though scheduling burdens limit adherence for some patients.
  • Combination of alitretinoin + NB-UVB is safe and may be synergistic in real-world practice .

Role of Contact Dermatitis

  • CHD frequently coexists with irritant or allergic contact dermatitis.
  • Allergen identification (e.g., methylisothiazolinone) may significantly improve outcomes.
  • Allergen avoidance may be sufficient for some patients. .

Treatment Algorithm Principles

  • Confirm diagnosis → educate on avoidance/moisturization → potent topicals → phototherapy or alitretinoin as second-line → consider combination strategies .
  • Assess adherence at every visit .

Impact on Work & Function

  • Effective therapy markedly reduces work impairment and disability, as supported by observational data .

Chronic hand eczema is a heterogeneous, relapsing inflammatory condition that can substantially impair function, sleep, and quality of life, and it frequently persists despite standard topical therapy, creating a common “refractory” clinical scenario . Contemporary reviews emphasize that chronic hand eczema is not a single disease entity; rather, it is an umbrella diagnosis that includes several subtypes (for example, irritant contact dermatitis, allergic contact dermatitis, atopic hand eczema, and hyperkeratotic phenotypes), which is one reason patients may respond variably to the same treatment approach .

Diagnostic approach and key tests to order

Diagnosis is primarily clinical and depends on lesion morphology (erythema, scale, vesiculation, fissuring), distribution, chronicity, and exposures; however, a structured work-up is recommended to avoid missing mimics and to correctly classify subtype . In practice, this includes (1) a complete skin examination to look for clues suggesting alternative or comorbid diagnoses (such as psoriasis or generalized atopic dermatitis), and to document extent and severity at baseline .

Because tinea manuum can resemble chronic hand eczema and can coexist with it, fungal testing (skin scraping for microscopy and/or fungal culture) is appropriate when the presentation is atypical, unilateral, treatment-refractory, or when scaling patterns raise suspicion for dermatophyte infection; excluding fungal infection is particularly important before escalating immunomodulatory therapy .

Patch testing is a central diagnostic step in chronic hand eczema, especially for persistent disease and in occupational contexts, because allergic contact dermatitis can masquerade as or perpetuate chronic hand eczema. Even when patch testing is negative, results help refine classification (for example, supporting irritant-predominant or idiopathic chronic hand eczema) and guide long-term avoidance counseling .

Foundational management: skin barrier, exposure reduction, and education

Across guidelines and reviews, nonpharmacologic management is consistently positioned as a foundation rather than an “add-on.” Regular use of emollients, avoidance of irritants and allergens (including wet work and harsh cleansers), and structured patient education are core components intended to reduce flares, improve tolerance of therapy, and support long-term disease control . This is especially relevant in chronic hand eczema because ongoing exposures can drive relapse even when anti-inflammatory therapy is effective.

Anti-inflammatory therapy and escalation for refractory disease

Topical corticosteroids remain a mainstay for flares, but chronic hand eczema often relapses when potent topical corticosteroids are tapered or stopped, prompting escalation strategies in moderate to severe disease . Phototherapy (for example, local psoralen plus ultraviolet A or narrowband ultraviolet B) is a guideline-supported treatment option for chronic hand eczema that remains refractory to topical therapy and can be effective within treatment algorithms .

For systemic options, the 2015 guideline evidence tables underscore important limitations and safety concerns for several immunosuppressive agents in hand eczema (for example, “no evidence” or lack of randomized controlled trial data for azathioprine and methotrexate in hand eczema, and recognized toxicities with cyclosporine; continuous systemic corticosteroid use is discouraged) . Recent reviews similarly highlight that commonly used systemic immunosuppressants are frequently off-label and constrained by toxicity monitoring and variable supporting evidence; for example, cyclosporine is associated with adverse events such as nephrotoxicity and hypertension and should be discontinued if ineffective after a defined period .

Overall, treatment of chronic hand eczema generally follows a stepwise escalation strategy based on disease severity and treatment response. Management typically begins with foundational measures such as emollient therapy, avoidance of irritants, and patient education, followed by potent topical corticosteroids for inflammatory disease. In patients with persistent or refractory symptoms, escalation to phototherapy or systemic therapy (for example, alitretinoin) may be considered. For individuals who do not respond adequately to conventional therapies or cannot tolerate systemic agents, newer targeted topical treatments such as Janus kinase inhibitors (e.g., delgocitinib cream) provide a steroid-sparing therapeutic option for long-term disease control (Diepgen TL et al., 2015; de León FJ et al., 2015; Gooderham M et al., 2017; Sheehan MP et al., 2023; Ghezzi G et al., 2025).

Emerging and newer topical options: delgocitinib cream

Topical Janus kinase inhibition is one of the most clinically mature “new mechanism” approaches in chronic hand eczema. Delgocitinib is described as a pan-Janus kinase inhibitor with activity across multiple inflammatory pathways relevant to hand eczema immunobiology, and clinical trials support meaningful efficacy in moderate to severe chronic hand eczema . In a 16-week randomized phase two dose-ranging study, delgocitinib cream demonstrated efficacy with a dose-dependent relationship, with commonly reported adverse events including headache and nasopharyngitis .

Importantly, phase three DELTA 1 and DELTA 2 trials (placebo-controlled, twice-daily delgocitinib cream) demonstrated significantly higher rates of treatment success (clear or almost clear by Investigator’s Global Assessment for Chronic Hand Eczema at week 16) compared with placebo; treatment success was achieved in 20% versus 10% in DELTA 1 and 29% versus 7% in DELTA 2 . These data support delgocitinib as a steroid-sparing option for patients with moderate to severe chronic hand eczema, including those with inadequate control on topical corticosteroids.
