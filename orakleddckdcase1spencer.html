Welcome. Your patient today is Spencer B.

Spencer is 64-year-old male patient with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD). He has been receiving in-center hemodialysis (HD) 3 times per week for 2 months. He has a history of type 2 diabetes, hypertension (HTN), and neuropathy. You are one of the main nurses who has been caring for Spencer. You ask if he is feeling more adjusted, because he was previously worried about being a burden to his family and potentially needing to take an early retirement because of his condition. He says his family is being very supportive. His daughters are helping to drive him to appointments if his wife is working, so he is feeling better about the transition.

At his last appointment you and the doctor talked with him about switching from a catheter to a fistula for HD access. When you bring it up today, he thanks you for getting him the information about it, but says he’s not feeling ready for another procedure yet. When you ask if there is something holding him back, he reveals that he hasn’t been sleeping well for the last few weeks and he’s been very tired with low energy and oddly he feels cold a lot of the time.

The patient was not started on an erythropoietin-stimulating agent (ESA) at HD initiation because his hemoglobin (Hb) level was adequate.

Case Review

This case highlights the importance of individualized treatment strategies in dialysis-dependent CKD. While ESAs remain the standard of care, newer agents like HIF-PHIs provide additional options for managing anemia. By focusing on symptom relief, patient education, and shared decision-making, Spencer’s care team ensured an outcome aligned with his needs and long-term dialysis goals.

About 1 in 7 or 35.5 million people in the United States have CKD . Among these, more than 820,000 have ESKD . People with ESKD, the most advanced stage of CKD, have significantly reduced HRQoL from the disease and/or dialysis itself . When anemia is present, HRQoL is further reduced (van Haalen et al., 2020). Many view the negative impact of anemia-related symptoms on HRQoL as the most detrimental effect of anemia of CKD . Therefore, a main clinical goal of anemia management in CKD is to improve symptoms .

As mentioned, anemia occurs in more than half of patients with ESKD (Stauffer & Fan, 2014). Anemia is associated with debilitating symptoms , including fatigue, weakness, shortness of breath, dizziness, headaches, and depression . In a multinational survey, 770 physicians provided data for 5276 patients, including 28% with CKD stage 4 and 36% on dialysis. Results showed significant associations between lower Hb levels and reduced HRQoL across numerous validated QoL tools and despite regression analyses adjusting for CKD stage and other potential confounders (van Haalen et al., 2020). Evidence has also shown that management to increase Hb levels may be associated with improved QoL in patients with CKD .

Anemia has been shown in observational studies to increase the risk of cardiovascular events, all-cause mortality, and CKD progression (the latter being more important in earlier stages of CKD) . Patients with ESKD have a 4- to 7-fold increased risk of mortality and 6- to 8-fold increased risk of cardiovascular mortality compared with people without CKD . About 75% of patients on dialysis (HD or PD) have been shown to have cardiovascular disease , which is the leading cause of death in ESKD .

Although assessing for and addressing anemia has become routine in CKD management, particularly within dialysis centers , it is important for the care team to recognize that anemia-related symptoms and manifestations are very burdensome to patients and can have a profound impact on their life experiences . In fact, it has been mandated in the United States that the care team within dialysis units routinely assess patient HRQoL, which spans several life domains . Although optimal management of CKD—including monitoring Hb levels—is critical, the link between anemia and patients’ overall well-being must be considered (van Haalen et al., 2020).

Diagnosis and Monitoring of DD-CKD Anemia

According to the KDIGO guidelines (2012), anemia should be diagnosed in people older than 15 years of age when Hb concentration is < 13 g/dL in males and < 12 g/dL in females, with Hb of 10 to 11 g/dL more clinically relevant for treatment . Once the diagnosis of anemia is established, causes of anemia are best assessed by measuring absolute reticulocyte count, serum ferritin, TSAT, serum vitamin B12, and folate .

The mean corpuscular volume (MCV) measures the volume of RBCs and categorizes anemia into microcytic (iron deficiency), macrocytic (vitamin B12 or folate deficiency), or normocytic anemia, which helps determine the underlying etiology (Maner & Sutter, 2024). The absolute reticulocyte count measures the number or percentage of immature RBCs and, when elevated, shows that the bone marrow is producing RBCs at an accelerated pace, typically from hemolysis or blood loss .

Iron deficiency, either absolute or functional, is common in patients with CKD . Indeed, more than half of patients with CKD have iron deficiency . Iron status is commonly assessed using TSAT and serum ferritin, where ferritin is the main iron storage protein and transferrin transports iron throughout the body .

Absolute Iron Deficiency

In CKD, the KDIGO guidelines (2012) recommend ferritin concentration ≤ 500 ng/mL and TSAT ≤ 30% to initiate iron therapy. Other recommendations diagnose absolute iron deficiency according to the dialysis type:

Hemodialysis (HD): Ferritin < 200 ng/mL and TSAT ≤ 20%

Peritoneal Dialysis (PD): Ferritin ≤ 100 ng/mL and TSAT ≤ 20% .

Functional Iron Deficiency

Functional iron deficiency occurs when total body iron stores are adequate, but the release of stored iron is not rapid enough, resulting in insufficient iron availability. When functional iron deficiency is present, TSAT is generally low (≤ 20%), whereas serum ferritin may be elevated . Thus, there is enough iron, but its availability is impaired.

The recommended frequency of Hb monitoring differs in patients with CKD with and without diagnosed anemia. Individualized Hb monitoring frequency is based on the CKD stage and dialysis status .

DD-CKD Anemia: Multifactorial Pathophysiology

The pathophysiology of anemia of CKD is multifactorial . Historically, the main recognized causes were:

Impaired erythropoiesis from decreased erythropoietin (EPO) production by the failing kidney

Changes in iron homeostasis .

A deeper understanding of its pathophysiology has shed light on other contributing factors, such as chronic inflammation, elevated hepcidin, and impaired detection of renal hypoxia . In addition, nutritional deficiencies (vitamin B12 and folate), hyperparathyroidism, the effects of uremia, which include diminished EPO production, increased blood loss, and shortened survival of RBCs, and the effects of dialysis itself (e.g., blood loss) are also implicated .

EPO

A relative lack of erythropoietin (EPO) remains the central cause of anemia of CKD. Erythropoiesis is driven by EPO and is the process by which red blood cells (RBCs) are produced. The kidneys are the main source of EPO production, and as kidney function declines, the kidneys no longer produce adequate amounts of EPO .

Failure of the kidneys to sense hypoxia through the hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) system also plays a role in reduced EPO production. HIF-α promotes EPO production and, under normoxic conditions, it is rapidly degraded. When tissue hypoxia (e.g., anemia) is recognized via the HIF system, HIF-α accumulates and binds to hypoxia response elements of the EPO gene, leading to increased EPO production . This process is impaired in CKD, contributing to EPO deficiency .
