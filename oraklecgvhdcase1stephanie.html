Case Review

This module presents a 53-year-old female patient with a history of ALL who developed cGVHD following an allogeneic BMT, leading to significant respiratory distress and notable skin findings, including hyperpigmentation and a chronic non-healing ulcer. The patient’s clinical course illustrates the heterogeneous and multisystem nature of cGVHD, which may evolve insidiously and involve overlapping inflammatory, fibrotic, and immune-mediated processes across multiple organs . Importantly, the case emphasizes that not all febrile episodes or pulmonary abnormalities in post-transplant patients are attributable to infection, as immune-mediated complications such as pulmonary cGVHD and bronchiolitis obliterans may present with nonspecific respiratory symptoms and imaging findings that mimic infectious etiologies . A multidisciplinary, guideline-driven approach was essential for accurate diagnosis and management, underscoring the need for early recognition, systematic exclusion of mimicking conditions—including infection, drug toxicity, and relapse—and coordinated care among hematology, pulmonology, dermatology, and supportive care teams .

GVHD is a severe immune-mediated complication that primarily occurs following allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), affecting approximately 40%–60% of recipients and contributing to approximately 15% of transplant-related mortality. Although it is most commonly associated with allogeneic HSCT, GVHD has also been reported in rare instances after transfusion of blood products, solid organ transplantation, and autologous HSCT. This underscores its complex immunological underpinnings and its potential to arise in diverse clinical contexts .

In the setting of allogeneic HSCT, the occurrence of GVHD is strongly influenced by human leukocyte antigen mismatch, the primary determinant of immune incompatibility. However, other factors, such as minor histocompatibility antigens, advanced recipient age, myeloablative conditioning regimens, donor-recipient gender disparity, donor multiparity, nonstandard GVHD prophylaxis, and the use of peripheral blood stem cells as the graft source, also contribute significantly. These diverse risk factors reflect the multifaceted nature of GVHD pathophysiology and highlight the challenges in optimizing prevention and treatment strategies .

Classification

The classification of cGVHD has evolved. Historically, GVHD was categorized as acute or chronic based solely on the timing of onset, with symptoms appearing within the first 100 days post-transplant considered acute, and those occurring afterward labeled chronic, regardless of clinical presentation. However, advances in transplantation practices, such as reduced-intensity conditioning regimens, donor lymphocyte infusions, and second allogeneic transplants, have blurred the timeline of GVHD onset. Additionally, relapse of acute GVHD symptoms can occur beyond 100 days, often linked to tapering or withdrawal of immunosuppression, necessitating a more nuanced approach to classification .

To address these complexities, the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Working Group introduced a revised classification system in 2005, based on clinical and histopathological features rather than timing alone. This system, further refined in the 2014 NIH Chronic GVHD Diagnosis and Staging Consensus , includes 4 categories: classic acute GVHD (≤100 days, with no chronic features), persistent, recurrent, or late-onset acute GVHD (>100 days, with no chronic features), classic cGVHD (no time limit, with chronic features only), and overlap syndrome (no time limit, with both acute and chronic features). The most recent 2020 NIH consensus update emphasized standardized diagnosis and early recognition of cGVHD, incorporating diagnostic and distinctive clinical features, functional assessments, and patient-reported outcomes to improve consistency in both clinical care and clinical trials

Pathophysiology

Chronic graft-versus-host disease (cGVHD) arises from a complex interplay of donor and recipient immune responses that leads to sustained alloreactivity, chronic inflammation, and progressive fibrosis. Following allogeneic HSCT, donor-derived T lymphocytes recognize host antigens as foreign, triggering a cascade of immune activation. Unlike acute GVHD, in which early T–cell–mediated cytotoxicity dominates. cGVHD pathogenesis involves both cellular and humoral immune dysregulation .

Key mechanisms include:

T-cell alloreactivity and cytokine release (e.g., IL-2, IFN-γ, TNF-α) drive chronic inflammation and tissue damage.

B-cell activation and autoantibody production that contribute to persistent immune-mediated injury, with increased BAFF (B-cell activating factor) levels observed in cGVHD patients .

Fibrosis and tissue remodeling resulting from dysregulated wound healing, with fibroblast activation and collagen deposition leading to sclerotic skin changes, bronchiolitis obliterans, and joint contractures .

This evolving understanding of cGVHD immunopathology has provided the rationale for emerging targeted therapies, including BTK inhibitors (ibrutinib), JAK inhibitors (ruxolitinib), and HDAC inhibitors (vorinostat), which specifically modulate B-cell signaling, cytokine pathways, and fibrotic responses .

Diagnosis

The diagnosis of chronic graft-versus-host disease (cGVHD) is based on diagnostic or distinctive clinical features across multiple organs, with supporting evidence from laboratory, imaging, or specialist evaluations. Diagnostic features are sufficient to confirm cGVHD, while distinctive features require further confirmation, such as biopsy or radiologic findings. Common features shared with acute GVHD are not specific for diagnosing cGVHD. The following are the criteria for diagnosis as proposed by the NIH Consensus Development Project :

According to the 2014 NIH Consensus Development Project , diagnostic criteria include:

Diagnostic features: these alone confirm cGVHD and include:

Skin: poikiloderma, sclerotic changes

Lungs: BO confirmed by biopsy

Mouth: lichen planus-like changes

Eyes: cicatricial conjunctivitis or keratoconjunctivitis sicca

GI tract: esophageal web or strictures

Genitalia: vaginal scarring, phimosis, or urethral stenosis

Distinctive features: require confirmation via biopsy, imaging, or lab tests. Examples:

Skin: depigmentation, ichthyosis

Lungs: BO syndrome diagnosed via PFT or imaging

Eyes: dry, gritty sensation without other causes

Mouth: xerostomia, mucosal atrophy

Liver: elevated bilirubin or alkaline phosphatase >2× upper normal

Exclusion of alternative etiologies: Infections, drug toxicities, or recurrent malignancy must be systematically excluded, as they may mimic or exacerbate cGVHD features.

Supporting Evidence:

Pulmonary function testing (FEV1, FVC, DLCO)

Schirmer’s test (for ocular involvement)

Radiographic imaging (e.g., HRCT for BO)

Specialist evaluations: ophthalmologist, gynecologist, or other relevant specialists

Biopsy: confirmatory when feasible, particularly for skin, mucosa, or lung involvement

Thus, the diagnosis of cGVHD requires a structured, multi-organ evaluation that balances clinical recognition of hallmark features with rigorous exclusion of mimicking conditions. This structured approach not only ensures specificity but also provides the foundation for appropriate staging and timely therapeutic intervention .

Global Scoring of cGVHD

The global scoring of cGVHD is based on the involvement and severity of 8 organs or sites: skin, mouth, eyes, GI tract, liver, lungs, joints and fascia, and genital tract. The scoring system categorizes severity as mild, moderate, or severe, reflecting the degree of organ impact and functional impairment :

Scoring Criteria:

Mild: 1–2 organs involved, no more than score 1 per organ, and a lung score of 0

Moderate: 3 or more organs involved, no more than score 1 per organ, or at least 1 organ with a score of 2 (excluding lungs), or lung score of 1

Severe: at least 1 organ with a score of 3, or lung score of 2 or 3

Key Features:

FEV1 is used to determine lung severity instead of a clinical score

If a non-GVHD cause unequivocally explains an organ abnormality, that organ is excluded from the global severity calculation

If organ abnormalities are multifactorial (GVHD and other causes), the score is entirely attributed to GVHD

Considerations:

The scoring system can only be applied after cGVHD is confirmed through diagnostic or distinctive features with supporting evidence (eg biopsy, imaging, or lab tests)

Asymptomatic manifestations, such as oral cGVHD, do not contribute to global severity (score remains 0)

Periodic reassessment of global scores is recommended for tracking disease progression and guiding treatment.

This scoring system provides a standardized framework for assessing cGVHD severity, allowing for accurate prognostication, treatment planning, and response evaluation. Confounders, such as non-GVHD causes of organ abnormalities, are carefully documented to improve specificity. This framework, first established by the NIH in 2005 and refined in the 2014/2015 consensus, was reaffirmed in the 2020 NIH update, which emphasized early recognition, systematic organ scoring, and integration of patient-reported outcomes .

Treatment

cGVHD treatment depends on severity and organ involvement. For mild cases, topical therapies like corticosteroids, CNIs, and moisturizers are effective for skin and oral lesions, while phototherapy is useful for extensive or steroid-refractory cases. According to ERS/EBMT 2024 guidelines, systemic corticosteroids remain the first-line therapy for moderate to severe cGVHD, with calcineurin and mTOR inhibitors used as adjuncts in selected cases. Ruxolitinib is recommended as the preferred second-line agent for steroid-refractory disease, while ibrutinib and belumosudil are also recognized as valid options in refractory settings. Extracorporeal photopheresis is endorsed as a steroid-sparing approach in steroid-dependent or refractory disease, particularly with pulmonary and cutaneous involvement. Structured rehabilitation, including pulmonary rehabilitation and physiotherapy, is recommended to preserve mobility and function. Other agents such as mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, and HDAC inhibitors (e.g., vorinostat) are considered investigational and are not guideline-standard .

In our patient, the suspicion of cGVHD was warranted, given her history of allogeneic BMT and the development of respiratory symptoms. Notably, symptoms such as dyspnea and pleuritic chest pain may be consistent with pulmonary cGVHD. In contrast, productive cough and fever are more typical of infection, underscoring the diagnostic overlap often encountered in this population . Additionally, the presence of chronic skin pigmentation and an ulcer on the left medial malleolus is supportive of cutaneous GVHD. These findings underline the necessity for clinicians to recognize that GVHD can manifest in various ways, often insidiously. The patient’s clinical presentation posed significant diagnostic challenges due to overlapping symptoms associated with infection and GVHD. Her leukocytosis and elevated inflammatory markers initially suggested a potential infectious process, particularly pneumonia, which is common in immunocompromised patients. The presence of bilateral pleural effusions on imaging further complicated the diagnosis, necessitating a comprehensive evaluation of both infectious and inflammatory causes.

The chronic skin pigmentation observed in the patient is particularly noteworthy. It should be regarded as compatible with cutaneous GVHD. Cutaneous manifestations may precede other systemic features of GVHD and can serve as an important diagnostic clue. In this patient, the skin findings, along with respiratory symptoms, create a compelling case for cGVHD as the underlying cause of her clinical deterioration. The initiation of corticosteroid therapy with prednisone led to a rapid improvement in the patient’s clinical status, consistent with established protocols for treating active disease of GVHD. While improvement in symptoms following steroid therapy supported the suspicion of GVHD, clinical or radiographic improvement alone should not be regarded as definitive proof of diagnosis; ongoing monitoring with pulmonary function tests and exclusion of infection remain essential . While corticosteroids are effective in managing GVHD, they also carry risks of immunosuppression, making patients susceptible to opportunistic infections. Therefore, careful monitoring is essential after starting steroid therapy to identify any complications that may arise.
