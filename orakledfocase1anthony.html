Introduction

History of Present Illness

Welcome. Your patient today is Anthony K.

A 54-year-old man with a history of type 2 diabetes presents with several weeks of worsening pain, swelling, and redness of his right foot. His symptoms began after noticing a small blister on the lateral plantar midfoot (near the 5th metatarsal base), which rapidly progressed into a painful ulcer with increasing warmth and tenderness. Over the past 10 days, he has had increasing difficulty bearing weight and reports low-grade fevers and malaise. He denies trauma, recent travel, or known peripheral vascular disease, but acknowledges poor glycemic control and inconsistent foot care. He reports that during a prior hospitalization for cellulitis, he developed a mild rise in creatinine while receiving antibiotics.

Despite outpatient oral antibiotics and ibuprofen started three days ago, his symptoms have worsened. He now has increased drainage from the ulcer and more pronounced swelling extending to the midfoot. He reports no numbness or new wounds elsewhere. The escalating pain, inability to walk without assistance, and persistent fever prompted him to seek evaluation.

Case Review

This case illustrates the complex diagnostic and therapeutic decision-making required in diabetic foot osteomyelitis (DFO), where early recognition of key clinical clues and thoughtful integration of imaging and microbiology are essential. The combination of a deep plantar ulcer, a positive probe-to-bone test, markedly elevated inflammatory markers, and radiographic evidence of cortical destruction and joint-space loss made osteomyelitis the most probable diagnosis even before advanced imaging was obtained. MRI subsequently confirmed extensive bone and soft-tissue involvement, including a sinus tract—findings that strongly support the diagnosis of severe DFO requiring systemic therapy.

A central theme of this case is the importance of pathogen-directed management. Deep-tissue culture grew MSSA in pure growth, providing a clear microbiologic target. The susceptibility pattern—resistant only to penicillin G and erythromycin and fully susceptible to oxacillin, cefazolin, doxycycline, TMP–SMX, fosfomycin, and vancomycin—guided the selection of appropriate therapy. Because effective DFO management hinges on matching treatment to the true causative organism, definitive therapy needed to shift away from broad empiric coverage toward targeted anti-staphylococcal agents with proven efficacy in osteomyelitis.

Several reasonable therapeutic choices were available. Narrow-spectrum β-lactams such as cefazolin or an anti-staphylococcal penicillin (“an oxacillin,” i.e., oxacillin, flucloxacillin, or cloxacillin, depending on local availability) represented guideline-supported monotherapy for MSSA osteomyelitis. In more extensive infections like this case, adjunctive combination regimens—such as adding IV fosfomycin, which achieves high bone concentrations—may be considered only as part of combination therapy (not monotherapy) to enhance tissue penetration and address high bacterial burden. Any acceptable regime, however, must be rooted in the susceptibility profile of the isolate, reinforcing the theme that microbiology results are the anchor for clinical decision-making in osteomyelitis.

Finally, the case underscores the need for a multidisciplinary approach. Given the extent of bone destruction and sinus tract formation, referral to orthopaedic surgery was appropriate to determine whether debridement or staged reconstruction might be required if medical therapy alone proves insufficient. Limb preservation in advanced DFO depends not only on correct antibiotic choice but also on coordinated surgical evaluation, vascular assessment when indicated, glycemic optimization, and off-loading strategies.
