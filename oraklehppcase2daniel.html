Introduction

A 56-Year-Old Male with Recurrent Fractures, Chronic Fatigue, and Persistent Foot Pain

History of Present Illness

Welcome. Your patient today is Daniel T

Daniel T, a 56-year-old male, presents with persistent lower extremity pain, progressive fatigue, and difficulty walking, which have significantly impacted his ability to maintain his full-time job as a warehouse manager. Over the years, he has experienced recurrent nontraumatic fractures in his feet and legs, which have been slow to heal. He reports self-medicating with over-the-counter calcium and vitamin D supplements, although without noticeable improvement. His symptoms have worsened over the last 6 months, leading to further reduced mobility and difficulty completing daily tasks.

He also reports early dental problems, including premature loss of teeth, which had been noted years before the onset of his current skeletal symptoms.

Daniel’s history of recurrent, nontraumatic fractures, early dental issues, coupled with his fatigue and family history, raises concern for an undiagnosed metabolic condition affecting bone health and connective tissue. Further diagnostic evaluation is crucial to identify contributing factors.

Family and Social History

Daniel reports a significant family history of musculoskeletal and chronic health conditions. His mother suffered with chronic joint pain and arthritis, managed with medication, and was treated for hypertension. Additionally, a maternal uncle experienced recurrent joint pain and suspected gout, while a paternal aunt had unexplained fatigue and progressive frailty, though no formal diagnosis was established. There is also a family history of osteoporosis and recurrent fractures, which may suggest underlying skeletal fragility in the family.

Daniel works reduced hours as a warehouse manager due to his symptoms of recurrent fractures, chronic fatigue, and persistent foot pain. He lives with his spouse and has 2 adult children who live independently. Previously active in physical activities and social engagements, He has significantly reduced his involvement in physical activities and social engagements due to his health issues. Daniel does not smoke, consumes alcohol occasionally (1–2 beers per week), and denies any history of illicit drug use. He reports difficulty maintaining an active lifestyle but attempts to follow a balanced diet.

Daniel’s history of recurrent, nontraumatic fractures, early dental issues, coupled with his fatigue and family history, raises concern for an undiagnosed metabolic condition affecting bone health and connective tissue. Further diagnostic evaluation is crucial to identify contributing factors.

Case Review

This case underscores the challenges and importance of diagnosing and managing a rare metabolic bone disorder like HPP. Daniel’s history of recurrent fractures, bone/joint pain and persistent fatigue, along with diagnostic findings such as persistently low ALP activity and imaging showing mataphyseal radiolucent areas, diffuse osteopenia with stress fractures, point to HPP. Consistently low activity of ALP are a notable concern, as they may signal an underlying metabolic condition like HPP .

Genetic testing was ordered to evaluate the underlying cause of his symptoms further. The test returned NM_000478.6(ALPL):c.571G>A (p.Glu191Lys), a pathogenic variant, confirming a mutation in the ALPL gene. This supports the diagnosis but is not essential for diagnosis of HPP, as his clinical and biochemical presentation already provides strong evidence.

Some patients with clinical and laboratory findings consistent with a diagnosis of HPP have negative genetic testing results.

Acknowledging the emotional impact of living with a rare, chronic disorder, Daniel’s experience underscores the significance of early recognition in understanding and managing rare metabolic bone disorders such as HPP.

HPP presents with a broad spectrum of symptoms, but the most common observed clinical features involve the skeleton, muscles, and teeth .

The skeletal symptoms of HPP include rickets leading to short stature and skeletal deformities such as bowed legs, a dolichocephalic skull with a "beaten copper" appearance, and enlarged wrists, knees, and ankles due to widened metaphyses. Radiographic findings may reveal focal bone defects, radiolucent "tongues" in long bones, and significant hypomineralization at the metaphyses of the distal ulna and proximal fibula .

Musculoskeletal manifestations of HPP include chronic pain, reduced mobility, and calcium pyrophosphate crystal deposition in joints, leading to conditions like chondrocalcinosis or pseudogout. Fractures and pseudofractures are also common, with 62.1% of patients experiencing at least one. Pseudofractures, or atypical femoral fractures (AFF), are a hallmark feature of HPP .

Age- and Sex-Adjusted ALP activity

Although the diagnosis of HPP is based on clinical evaluation, considering the signs, symptoms, and associated complications of the condition, age- and sex-adjusted ALP activity are critical in diagnosis, as persistently low ALP activity is a hallmark biochemical marker of the condition .

Age- and sex-adjusted reference ranges for alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity are crucial for diagnosing HPP, particularly during growth periods when ALP activity fluctuates significantly. ALP activity generally rises slightly in childhood, peaks during periods of rapid growth, and declines after puberty. Sex-specific differences in ALP activity between ages 11 and 79 reflect unique bone remodeling patterns across life stages . Additional biochemical markers, such as elevated PLP and urinary phosphoethanolamine (PEA), further support the diagnosis, providing a comprehensive biochemical profile to confirm HPP.

Age- and Sex-Wise Trends in ALP Activity

The diagnosis of HPP relies on persistently low ALP activity adjusted for age and sex, as ALP activity varies significantly throughout life. In children and adolescents, ALP activity is influenced by bone growth, peaking during periods of rapid growth and declining after puberty. Any disorder impairing linear growth can reduce ALP activity, making it essential to benchmark measurements against age- (or bone age, if delayed) and sex-specific reference ranges .

Pediatric trends: ALP activity closely reflects patterns of bone growth, typically rising moderately during childhood and peaking during periods of rapid growth. After puberty, as growth slows and eventually ceases, ALP activity gradually decreases . Tables depicting age-based trends in ALP activity provide a valuable diagnostic tool, allowing clinicians to evaluate enzyme activity relative to expected norms for age and growth .

Adult trends: ALP activity stabilizes at lower values after skeletal maturation . Further increases are observed in older adults, particularly postmenopausal women . Geographic variations also play a role, with baseline ALP activity differing between populations due to regional differences in lifestyle and health factors.

Sex-specific differences: the relationship between ALP and sex reveals distinct patterns across age groups. Before the age of 50, ALP marker activity is generally higher in men than women, consistent with findings from earlier studies. However, at around 50, ALP activity increase in women, coinciding with menopause, while remaining relatively unchanged in men .

The age-, sex-, and geography-dependent nature of ALP activity highlights the importance of using tailored reference ranges and diagnostic tools, such as percentile graphs, in clinical practice. Persistently low ALP activity, characteristic of conditions like HPP, can only be reliably identified when these variations are accounted for, ensuring accurate diagnosis.

Diagnosis of HPP in Adults with Skeletal Symptoms

HPP in adults often presents with skeletal symptoms that can overlap with more common conditions, necessitating a thorough and systematic diagnostic approach. In adulthood, HPP is characterized by musculoskeletal pain, recurrent fractures (commonly in the feet, femur, and hip), slow healing, and occasional pseudofractures or bowing deformities. Other skeletal issues include calcific periarthritis, chondrocalcinosis, pseudogout, variable bone mineral density, and early tooth loss. Osteomalacia-related changes, such as deformities and weak bones, are also common . In severe cases, these symptoms may significantly impact mobility and daily activities." .

Key diagnostic steps begin with measuring ALP activity adjusted for age and sex, as persistently low activity is a defining feature of HPP. Supporting biochemical markers such as elevated PLP and urinary PEA further strengthen the diagnosis . To ensure diagnostic accuracy, it is essential to rule out other causes of skeletal symptoms, including osteoporosis, Paget disease, and osteogenesis imperfecta.

Confirmatory testing may involve genetic analysis to identify mutations in the ALPL gene, alongside radiographic studies to detect skeletal abnormalities and confirm fracture patterns .
