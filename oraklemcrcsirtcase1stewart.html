Introduction

A Patient With Metastatic Colorectal Cancer

History of Present Illness

Welcome. Your patient today is Stewart O.

A 46 year-old male patient was diagnosed with metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC) of the transverse colon after routine colonoscopy. He initially pursued alternative medical therapies but presented several months later with colonic obstruction from his primary lesion. Surgical resection was attempted but abandoned due to tumor involvement with the superior mesenteric vein and mesentery, and a colonic bypass was performed.

The patient began FOLFOX (oxaliplatin, 5-fluorouracil [5-FU], leucovorin) chemotherapy. After the first cycle (of a planned 6 cycles), the hepatic metastases and primary lesion decreased in size, and FOLFOX was continued. After the second cycle of therapy, the hepatic metastases increased in size and number.

A staging assessment was performed based on imaging findings. The patient’s disease was classified as cT4b cN2 cM1a (stage IV mCRC), given the primary tumor’s invasion into the superior mesenteric vein and mesentery, regional nodal involvement, and extensive hepatic metastases. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and positron emission tomography (PET) imaging revealed approximately 17 metastatic lesions involving two hepatic lobes, with larger lesions (>1.5 cm) demonstrating central necrosis and peripheral rim enhancement, indicating significant liver tumor burden. Given the unresectable nature of the liver metastases, further systemic therapy was pursued.

The patient underwent additional systemic chemotherapy treatments, receiving 4 cycles of an irinotecan-based regimen with cetuximab and bevacizumab.

He presents today with worsening abdominal discomfort, jaundice, and fatigue.

Case Review

SIRT has been extensively studied for its role in managing chemotherapy-refractory liver-dominant mCRC. This treatment involves injecting radioactive microspheres into the hepatic artery to deliver targeted radiation to liver metastases while sparing normal liver tissue. SIRT has been explored as a standalone treatment, as part of multimodal therapy, and in comparison with best supportive care (BSC) .

A matched-pair study evaluated OS in patients with chemotherapy-refractory liver-dominant mCRC treated with SIRT plus BSC compared to BSC alone. The study included 29 patients in each group and found that SIRT significantly prolonged median survival (8.3 months vs 3.5 months, p < 0.001), with a hazard ratio of 0.3 (95% confidence interval: 0.16–0.55). These findings highlight the potential of SIRT in extending survival in patients who have exhausted systemic treatment options .

The use of SIRT in combination with chemotherapy has also been explored. A phase 3 trial compared protracted intravenous 5-FU infusion alone versus 5-FU with Y-90 resin microspheres in patients with liver-limited mCRC refractory to standard chemotherapy. The addition of SIRT resulted in a significant increase in PFS, reinforcing its role as a viable adjunct to systemic therapy for improving disease control in chemotherapy-resistant patients .

Patient selection is crucial for optimizing SIRT outcomes. Candidates should have preserved liver function (bilirubin <1.75 mg/dL, AST, ALT, and ALP within safe limits) and ECOG Performance Status of 0 or 1. Additionally, hepatic arterial anatomy should allow safe microsphere delivery, and lung shunt assessments are required to prevent excessive radiation exposure to the lungs. Patients with liver-dominant disease and no major extrahepatic progression derive the most benefit from this treatment .

The safety profile of SIRT has been well documented, with adverse events generally being mild to moderate. In the matched-pair study, the most common side effects were grade 1–2 fatigue (69%), grade 1 abdominal pain or nausea (48.3%), and grade 2 gastrointestinal ulceration (10.3%). Three cases (10.3%) of grade 3 radiation-induced liver disease were reported, all of which were symptomatically managed. These findings support SIRT as a well-tolerated therapy when patients are carefully selected and monitored .

Imaging techniques play a crucial role in evaluating SIRT response. Standard RECIST may not fully capture treatment effects due to necrosis and peritumoral edema, which can cause an apparent increase in tumor size. PET/CT imaging is recommended for assessing metabolic response, as it provides a clearer picture of viable tumor activity post-SIRT .

SIRT has been compared with other liver-directed therapies such as radiofrequency ablation (RFA) and transarterial chemoembolization (TACE). While RFA is limited to small tumors (<3 cm) and TACE primarily serves as a palliative measure, SIRT has demonstrated superior disease control for patients with extensive liver metastases. Given its ability to deliver targeted high-dose radiation with manageable toxicity, SIRT is a valuable option for patients with liver-dominant mCRC who have failed chemotherapy .
