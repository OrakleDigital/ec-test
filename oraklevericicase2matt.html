Introduction

A 52-Year-Old African American Male with End-Stage Renal Disease Secondary to Diabetes Mellitus and Hypertension

History of Present Illness

Welcome. Your patient today is Matt P.

A 52-year-old African American male with a history of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) due to diabetes mellitus and hypertension received a crossmatch-negative deceased donor kidney transplant. The donor's kidney was from a donation after circulatory death with a Kidney Donor Profile Index (KDPI) of 28%. The patient had a panel reactive antibody (PRA) of 60%, comprising class I PRA 60% and class II PRA 0%. He was monitored post-transplant for the development of de novo donor-specific antibodies (DSAs). He underwent induction therapy with thymoglobulin. Following the transplant, he experienced delayed graft function (DGF) with anuria, requiring continued hemodialysis at the time of discharge. He was discharged on extended-release tacrolimus Envarsus and mycophenolate mofetil (MMF). Notably, corticosteroids were not included in the maintenance immunosuppression regimen.

Family and Social History

The patient is a married warehouse supervisor living with his spouse and two adult children. He is a former smoker, drinks alcohol occasionally, and denies drug use. His diet is diabetic friendly but challenging to maintain due to work demands, and his physical activity is limited by fatigue and dialysis. Family history includes hypertension and stroke in his father, diabetes and kidney disease in his mother, and a sibling with diabetes. He has strong family support, relies on them for transportation at times, and has insurance but worries about missing work for medical procedures.

Physical Examination Notes

The patient is conscious, alert, and well oriented x3. He appears well nourished and in no acute distress. Cardiovascular and pulmonary exams are unremarkable. The abdomen is soft with mild tenderness over the transplant site but no signs of complications. No swelling, or signs of infection or rejection are noted at the transplant site. Extremities are normal, and the neurologic exam is intact.

Case Review

Managing patients with delayed graft function (DGF) presents a complex challenge for healthcare professionals due to the ambiguity surrounding the underlying cause. The potential reasons for DGF include persistent acute tubular necrosis (ATN), transplant rejection, or other contributing factors. Unlike patients with stable kidney function, whose progress can be monitored effectively through urine output and serum creatinine levels, those experiencing DGF exhibit a complete lack of renal function. This absence renders these clinical indicators unreliable for detecting possible rejection. The emergence of early biomarkers that can accurately identify or rule out rejection would significantly enhance patient care, potentially alleviating the need for invasive procedures such as biopsies. In this specific case, a renal biopsy was performed 3 to 4 weeks following the transplantation. The findings from the biopsy showed a correlation with Tutiiva scores, highlighting the possibility that the biopsy could have been postponed without compromising patient safety or care outcomes.

In managing this case of DGF due to ATN following deceased donor kidney transplantation, multiple therapeutic approaches were considered. Given the absence of biopsy findings consistent with acute rejection and the ongoing need for dialysis, the focus remained on supportive care, immunosuppressive optimization, and close monitoring of renal function.

While alternative strategies such as empirical rejection treatment or early biopsy-driven adjustments to immunosuppression could be valid, the approach taken prioritized histological confirmation of ATN before modifying therapy. However, Tutivia™ testing could be pivotal in guiding early immunosuppressive decision-making. The patient’s second Tutivia™ result of 28, obtained during the second post-transplant week, confirmed persistently low rejection risk . This could allow the clinical team to initiate early tacrolimus dose reduction with or without adding extra agents such as prednisone or belatacept, helping balance rejection prevention with minimizing potential nephrotoxicity while carefully and serially monitoring the Tutivia scores.

By offering non-invasive transcriptomic insights into immune activation, Tutivia™ enabled early, personalized intervention and delayed or potentially avoided the need for biopsy, depending on clinical progression. Additional supportive measures, including fluid balance monitoring, dietary optimization, and physical rehabilitation, are emphasized to enhance graft recovery and patient outcomes.

This case highlights the value of individualized post-transplant care, integrating biopsy-based and biomarker-driven diagnostics to optimize treatment decisions, reduce unnecessary interventions, and minimize complications.

DGF in Deceased Donor Kidney Transplants

DGF is a form of AKI that occurs post-transplant, particularly in recipients of deceased donor kidneys. The incidence of DGF has been increasing over time, despite advances in acute rejection management. Between 1985 and 1992, the reported DGF rate in US transplant registries was 14.7%, which increased to 23% between 1998 and 2004. Studies also indicate a DGF incidence of 21.3% in US kidney transplant recipients in 2008. The rising prevalence of DGF is largely attributed to the increased utilization of donation after circulatory death and expanded criteria donor kidneys, both of which have higher rates of ischemia-reperfusion injury .

DGF and Immunosuppression Management

Induction immunosuppression may help reduce DGF by suppressing vascular congestion and endothelial injury. Studies show that DGF rates are consistently higher in patients who do not receive induction therapy, with commonly used agents including anti-CD25 monoclonal antibodies, anti-thymocyte globulin (ATG), and anti-CD52 (alemtuzumab). While DGF rates vary, trials suggest comparable outcomes between basiliximab (20 mg) and cumulative ATG doses of 6–9 mg/kg, with higher ATG doses potentially lowering DGF rates in high-risk patients .

For maintenance immunosuppression, withholding CNIs post-transplant does not appear to improve DGF outcomes. Trials show that delaying tacrolimus until postoperative day 7 resulted in a higher DGF rate (30.3% vs 23.8%), while cyclosporine administration on postoperative day 0 versus day 6 yielded similar DGF rates (26.8% vs 23.0%). Additionally, low tacrolimus trough levels (<9 ng/mL) by postoperative day 5 were associated with an increased risk of acute rejection, highlighting the need to maintain CNI therapy during early graft recovery to balance rejection risk and optimize outcomes .

The Role of Biomarkers in Risk Stratification

Biomarkers have gained increasing attention as non-invasive tools for assessing the cause of DGF and guiding post-transplant management. Serum creatinine alone lacks specificity and can be influenced by multiple factors unrelated to rejection. The use of biomarkers such as dd-cfDNA and DSAs has been proposed to improve risk stratification and treatment decisions .

DSAs and Rejection Risk

DSAs are critical biomarkers for assessing AMBR and guiding post-transplant management. Guidelines recommend obtaining a serum sample at the time of renal biopsy for graft dysfunction to test for Human Leukocyte Antigen (HLA)-specific antibodies, as the presence of DSA in conjunction with biopsy findings strengthens the diagnosis of ABMR .

Tutivia™ Score Interpretation in Risk Assessment and Clinical Decision-Making

The Tutivia™ score is a blood-based transcriptomic RNA expression test, which utilizes a proprietary AI algorithm to assess the risk of acute rejection in kidney transplant recipients, correlating with Banff 2022 histological findings. The risk score ranges from 0 to 100, with a predetermined cut-point of 50 distinguishing low risk (<50) from high risk (≥50) patients .

In validation studies, 26.5% of patients had a high-risk score, with 60% correlating with rejection on biopsy, while 73.5% had a low-risk score, with 79% showing no histologic rejection. The receiver operating characteristic curve demonstrated an area under the curve of 0.693 (P<0.001), indicating moderate predictive accuracy. By providing a non-invasive risk assessment, Tutivia™ assists clinicians in making treatment decisions, helping to avoid unnecessary immunosuppression in low-risk patients while identifying high-risk patients who may require further evaluation. .

Clinical Implications of Biomarker-Based Strategies

Utilizing biomarkers such as Tutivia™ and DSA testing has clinical and economic benefits, as it can prevent unnecessary treatment escalation and guide personalized patient management. Avoiding unnecessary immunosuppression reduces the risks of drug toxicity, infection, and adverse metabolic effects, while still ensuring that rejection cases are promptly identified and treated .
