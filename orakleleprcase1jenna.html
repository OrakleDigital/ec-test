Introduction

History of Present Illness

Welcome. Your patient today is Jenna M.

A 7-year-old girl was brought by her parents for evaluation of severe early-onset obesity and constant hunger. Parents report excessive weight gain since infancy, with her weight crossing above the 97th percentile before age 2 and remaining >99th percentile for BMI thereafter, with rapid progression despite multiple visits to pediatricians and dietitians and implementation of a strict eating regimen and lifestyle measures. She has markedly increased hunger with poor satiety, repeatedly asking for food shortly after meals and remaining very food-focused throughout the day. They must closely supervise access to food; on occasion, she has sneaked extra food (e.g., spreads at breakfast).

Despite some reduction and later stabilization of BMI with these measures, she remains in the severe obesity (grade 3) range. She sleeps through the night without loud snoring, witnessed apneas, or gasping, and there is no history of restless sleep or frequent awakenings. Her physical activity is limited to routine school activities; outside school, she is mostly sedentary and spends ~2–3 hours per day in screen-based activities (TV/tablet).

Behaviorally, she exhibits aggressive/disruptive behaviors and socialization difficulties, and school reports significant problems with learning and classroom integration, such that she struggles to maintain a normal attitude and functioning at school. She also has a history of recurrent lower respiratory tract infections in early childhood (e.g., bronchitis/pneumonia), requiring multiple medical visits and courses of antibiotics. There is no known history of pituitary hormone deficiencies, hyperinsulinemia, or frequent infections, and no signs of puberty have been observed. The parents feel judged by others who suggest they are simply overfeeding her and are now seeking further evaluation for a possible underlying medical cause of her persistent hyperphagia and obesity.

Family and Social History

The patient is the first child of healthy, non-consanguineous parents and lives at home with her parents and a younger sister. There is no known family history of severe, early-onset obesity or similar eating behavior; parents and relatives are not described as having syndromic obesity. The parents are highly involved in her care and have implemented a strict eating regimen and lifestyle structure for the whole family. They report significant psychosocial stress around food management and describe feeling judged by others, including health professionals and strangers, who often assume they are “overfeeding” their daughter despite their efforts.

Physical examination notes

Height is in the low–normal range (Z –1.22; 11th percentile) and within her target height range; weight and BMI are >99th percentile (Z 2.53), consistent with severe (grade 3) obesity, with generalized adiposity. Blood pressure is 102/64 mmHg, within the normal range for age and height. No dysmorphic features are noted. There are no clinical signs of pituitary hormone deficiency, no evidence of puberty, and the remainder of the examination is unremarkable.

Case Review

This case illustrates the classic phenotype of leptin receptor (LEPR) deficiency: severe early-onset obesity, relentless hyperphagia, and rapid crossing of weight centiles in the first years of life. Similar to other reported LEPR-deficient children, Jenna’s leptin concentration is elevated for age and adiposity, indicating preserved leptin production but impaired receptor-mediated signalling rather than congenital leptin deficiency .

Genetic testing confirmed biallelic pathogenic LEPR variants, consistent with the autosomal recessive pattern and functional loss-of-function described in mechanistic studies, where mutant receptors show reduced surface expression, impaired leptin binding, and blunted STAT3 activation . This molecular diagnosis provides a unifying explanation for her hyperphagia, severe obesity, and evolving metabolic risk, and clearly distinguishes her condition from more common polygenic or syndromic forms of obesity . A clear diagnosis can also reduce stigma, validate the family’s experience of “out-of-control” hunger, and support personalised, long-term management planning.

Leptin receptor (LEPR) deficiency is a rare autosomal recessive form of monogenic obesity caused by loss-of-function variants impairing leptin signaling in the hypothalamic leptin–melanocortin pathway. Since the first human cases were reported, LEPR deficiency has been recognized as an important cause of severe early-onset obesity with intense hyperphagia, typically presenting in infancy or early childhood . The phenotype overlaps clinically with congenital leptin (LEP) deficiency, but key biochemical and genetic differences allow reliable distinction between these disorders.

Epidemiology

Monogenic forms of obesity are rare but potentially treatable causes of severe early-onset obesity. In large pediatric cohorts with early-onset severe obesity, the overall prevalence of genetic (monogenic) obesity is estimated at around 7%, with even higher rates reported in populations with a high degree of consanguinity (e.g., Pakistani families) . More than 60 genes have now been associated with nonsyndromic monogenic obesity, most of them inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern, and defects in the leptin–melanocortin pathway account for the majority of known cases . In the landmark NEJM cohort, pathogenic LEPR mutations were identified in 3% (8/300) of children with hyperphagia and severe early-onset obesity, underscoring that LEPR deficiency is rare overall but enriched in highly selected early-onset, severe cases .

Pathophysiology of Leptin–Melanocortin Signaling

Leptin is an adipocyte-derived hormone that acts on the central nervous system to regulate energy balance. Within the hypothalamus, leptin binds to the long signaling isoform of the leptin receptor (LEPR-b), initiating intracellular pathways that regulate appetite and satiety. Functional studies confirm that LEPR activation normally stimulates STAT3 phosphorylation, a key component of leptin’s intracellular signaling cascade .

Dubern and Clément describe the leptin–melanocortin pathway as a network in which leptin activates POMC/CART neurons and inhibits NPY/AGRP neurons that otherwise promote feeding; activation of POMC neurons increases α-MSH, which in turn stimulates melanocortin-4 receptors (MC4R) to promote satiety and reduce food intake .

Effect of LEPR Deficiency on the Pathway

In LEPR deficiency, this upstream regulatory step fails because the receptor cannot transmit the leptin signal:

Loss of receptor signaling: LEPR variants show blunted or abolished STAT3 activation, confirming failure of intracellular signaling despite leptin binding attempts .

Leptin cannot activate POMC neurons or suppress NPY/AGRP neurons, so α-MSH production is reduced, and MC4R neurons are insufficiently activated .

As a result, the melanocortin pathway remains functionally under-stimulated, mimicking a state of energy deprivation.

Physiological Consequences

Although circulating leptin is normal for fat mass, the brain behaves as if leptin were absent. In the NEJM cohort, children with biallelic LEPR mutations exhibited hyperphagia from early childhood onward with rapid weight gain, despite leptin levels appropriate for their adiposity . This creates a physiological state of perceived starvation, leading to relentless hunger, reduced satiety, and severe early-onset obesity.

Genetics and Molecular Pathophysiology

LEPR loss-of-function variants include missense, nonsense, frameshift, splice-site changes, and large deletions. Functional data confirm that many of these variants impair receptor trafficking, reduce cell-surface expression, diminish leptin binding, or blunt downstream phosphorylation of STAT3 . As a result, individuals are unable to respond appropriately to circulating leptin despite normal or high serum leptin concentrations.

Farooqi and colleagues established the clinical and molecular spectrum of LEPR deficiency, demonstrating that individuals with biallelic LEPR mutations exhibit hyperphagia beginning early in life, rapid weight gain, and varying degrees of hypothalamic–pituitary dysfunction . These findings provide a coherent mechanistic explanation for the observed phenotype and support the use of targeted sequencing in any child presenting with severe early-onset hyperphagia and obesity without syndromic features, as next-generation sequencing approaches—including whole-exome and targeted gene panel sequencing—have been shown to successfully identify pathogenic LEPR variants in affected families

Clinical Features

1. Severe early-onset obesity and hyperphagia

Across all major series, LEPR deficiency presents with severe obesity beginning in infancy and intense, persistent hyperphagia—often requiring strict external control of food access . Hyperphagia is frequently described as relentless and behaviourally disruptive, with impaired satiety and rapid return of hunger after meals. The Wellinga patient narrative provides a contemporary description of this phenotype, emphasizing increased hunger, impaired satiety, food-seeking behaviours, and extreme difficulty in maintaining dietary structure despite family effort .

2. Endocrine abnormalities

LEPR deficiency is frequently associated with hypothalamic–pituitary axis abnormalities, particularly hypogonadotropic hypogonadism and central hypothyroidism . Puberty may be delayed or incomplete, although spontaneous pubertal development has been described in some cases . Hyperinsulinemia and insulin resistance develop early as consequences of severe obesity .

3. Immune and reproductive effects

Alterations in immune function and increased infection rates have been described, consistent with leptin’s role in T-cell signaling . Reproductive function in LEPR deficiency is variable, largely reflecting underlying hypogonadotropic hypogonadism. However, fertility is not necessarily impaired, and successful natural pregnancies have been documented .

4. Neurobehavioral manifestations and infectious morbidity

Beyond hyperphagia and endocrine dysfunction, recent data emphasize that biallelic LEPR deficiency frequently presents with significant neurobehavioral impairment and increased infectious morbidity. In the largest cohort reported to date, children with congenital leptin or leptin receptor deficiency commonly exhibited cognitive impairment, slow learning, socialization difficulties, aggressive/disruptive behaviors, and high rates of school dropout, indicating that LEPR deficiency may extend beyond a purely metabolic phenotype . Moreover, affected children demonstrated very high rates of life-threatening morbidity and premature mortality, predominantly due to severe respiratory and gastrointestinal infections, consistent with leptin’s essential role in immune regulation . These observations are consistent with the original report by Clément et al. (1998), which described severe early-onset hyperphagic obesity accompanied by marked food-seeking behavior, including “fighting with other children for food,” supporting the presence of disruptive/aggressive behavior in LEPR deficiency.

Leptin Physiology and Diagnostic Utility

A central diagnostic feature is that individuals with LEPR deficiency have leptin levels that are appropriate for their degree of adiposity, unlike congenital LEP deficiency, where leptin levels are very low or undetectable (Farooqi IS et al., 2007; von Schnurbein J et al., 2024). Because leptin production is intact but receptor signaling is impaired, circulating leptin concentrations do not appear disproportionately elevated relative to BMI. Measurement of serum leptin is therefore a useful early discriminant when evaluating severe early-onset obesity and hyperphagia.

The use of leptin SDS charts enables clinicians to interpret leptin levels relative to age, sex, pubertal stage, and BMI. In LEPR deficiency, leptin generally tracks within the expected SDS range .

Distinguishing LEPR Deficiency From Other Monogenic Obesity Syndromes

LEPR deficiency shares early-onset hyperphagia and severe obesity with other disorders in the leptin–melanocortin pathway, but specific associated features help to distinguish between etiologies:

LEP deficiency: undetectable leptin levels; dramatic response to leptin replacement therapy .

POMC & PCSK1 deficiency: adrenal insufficiency, hypoglycemia, cholestasis, or characteristic pigmentation changes .

MC4R deficiency: severe early-onset obesity but no hypothalamic–pituitary dysfunction .

Syndromic obesity (e.g., Bardet–Biedl): additional ophthalmologic, renal, or neurologic features not present in LEPR deficiency (Forsyth R. & Gunay-Aygun M., 2023).

Accurate differentiation is essential because therapeutic strategies differ across conditions.

Therapeutic validation of LEPR deficiency

The ultimate confirmation that LEPR mutations are causative of severe obesity comes from successful pharmacologic rescue of the downstream melanocortin pathway. The first human cases of leptin receptor deficiency described by Clément et al. (1998) established LEPR loss-of-function as a disorder characterized by severe obesity developing within the first months of life and marked hyperphagic food-seeking behavior. Subsequent clinical studies demonstrated that MC4R agonist therapy (setmelanotide) induces sustained reductions in hyperphagia and body weight in individuals with biallelic LEPR mutations, including patients in whom bariatric surgery had failed, thereby providing functional and therapeutic validation of the disease mechanism first identified in the original family .

Summary

LEPR deficiency is a rare but distinctly recognizable monogenic cause of severe early-onset obesity and hyperphagia. The combination of early and intense hyperphagia, severe obesity before age five, normal leptin concentrations, and hypothalamic–pituitary abnormalities should prompt targeted genetic testing. Advances in functional characterization and pathway-specific therapies, particularly MC4R agonists, are rapidly transforming prognosis for affected individuals. Early diagnosis supports personalized management, structured metabolic monitoring, and informed family counseling, reducing stigma and improving long-term outcomes.
