INTRODUCTION

TS is a chromosomal disorder characterized by the partial or complete absence of one of the X chromosomes in females. It affects approximately 25–50 per 100,000 females (≈1 in 2000–4000 live female births) and presents with a range of clinical features, including short stature, delayed puberty, and a range of potential organ anomalies and health risks.

EPIDEMIOLOGY

TS may remain undiagnosed until later in life . The syndrome is often associated with spontaneous miscarriage or stillbirth in utero, with a high percentage of affected fetuses not surviving to term. Karyotype analysis shows that TS results from the complete or partial absence of one of the X chromosomes, with about 40–50% of cases involving a 45,X karyotype (monosomy X). Mosaicism, including 45,X/46,XX, accounts for approximately 15–25% of cases and is often associated with a milder phenotype.

SYMPTOMS AND CLINICAL PRESENTATION

Short stature, defined as height exceeding 2 SDs below the mean, is a hallmark of TS, often identified in early childhood. But some children have normal stature until about 11 years of age. In older patients, the presenting symptom is typically related to delayed puberty. Patients usually show low or absent breast development (only about 13% show spontaneous breast development) and amenorrhea but normal pubic hair growth.

High-arched palate

Dental crowding or malocclusion

The severity of TS can vary significantly. For example, mosaic cases (eg 45,X/46,XX) generally present with milder symptoms.vi Individuals with TS may have a higher risk of congenital heart disease, renal anomalies, and hearing issues. In particular, girls with TS and horseshoe kidneys face an elevated risk of Wilms’ tumor and should undergo regular monitoring by renal ultrasound.

Phenotypic severity varies widely and does not correlate perfectly with karyotype, necessitating individualized assessment and lifelong surveillance regardless of mosaic status .

DIAGNOSIS

The diagnosis of TS is confirmed through karyotyping, which identifies a missing or partially missing X chromosome. Microarray or FISH analysis can also assist in diagnosis.

Karyotyping with analysis of at least 30 metaphases is recommended as the first-line diagnostic test. Microarray, FISH, or PCR may be used when rapid results are required, but standard karyotyping remains the confirmatory test .

These patients can have multiple comorbidities, and, before starting treatment, it is essential to assess the following:

Where Y chromosome material is detected, individualized decision-making regarding gonadectomy is recommended, balancing malignancy risk against potential gonadal function

Gonadotropin levels: these are elevated in patients with TS under 4 years of age. These hormone levels then stabilize to normal or near-normal values, only to rise again to menopausal levels after age 10 .

Thyroid function tests: TS patients have a high prevalence of hypothyroidism .

Glucose metabolism: HbA1c measurements should start at age 10 and continue into adulthood.

Celiac screen: it is advised to evaluate tTg IgA, as celiac disease can be associated with TS.

Urine culture, serum urea nitrogen, and creatinine: it is recommended these are tested in patients with renal obstruction .

Anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH): should be measured from diagnosis and monitored annually when fertility preservation is considered, as it predicts ovarian reserve and likelihood of spontaneous puberty .

Additional diagnostic tests include fasting lipids, liver enzymes, and a CBC with electrolytes to rule out other systemic disorders, such as anemia.

Imaging studies at diagnosis include:

A renal and collecting system ultrasound

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) at diagnosis in all patients

Cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) in all adolescents and adults newly diagnosed with TS, ideally within 12 months, or earlier if echocardiography is incomplete

Bone age, to assess before starting estrogen therapy

Bone density, if the patient is an adult and then 5 years after hormone replacement discontinuation; DXA after completion of growth and before age 21, then every 5–10 years in adulthood, or earlier in high-risk patients

Hearing assessment every 3 years and more frequently in patients with recurrent otitis media

TREATMENT

GH Therapy

The administration of GH therapy is a key component in managing TS, helping to increase final adult height. Higher doses of GH, especially during the first year of treatment, have been shown to result in improved growth outcomes . Initiating GH therapy at 4–6 years old is ideal, as later diagnoses are linked to lower final height and potential psychosocial impacts . Guidelines recommend offering GH early because growth failure begins before birth and is rapid in the first years of life, and treatment may be offered from as young as 2 years in the presence of growth failure/short stature or likelihood of short stature . For girls diagnosed later, treatment can still yield height gains of approximately 6 cm with 3 years of GH therapy. Dose optimization is crucial, with higher doses often needed to achieve significant growth responses. A recommended starting dose is 45–50 μg/kg/day, with escalation up to a maximum of 68 μg/kg/day if response is suboptimal and/or adult height potential remains substantially compromised .

GH therapy may be continued until little growth potential remains (bone age ≥14 years and/or height velocity <2 cm/year) .

Response should be monitored by measuring height at least every 6 months, and IGF-I should be measured at least annually; IGF-I is generally maintained within the normal range for age/pubertal stage/sex, with dose reduction considered for persistently high IGF-I values .

Estrogen Therapy

Estrogen therapy is essential for promoting sexual development and preventing osteoporosis in TS patients . Starting low-dose TD estradiol at 11 years, with gradual increases over 2–3 years, is recommended to mimic natural puberty. Guidelines recommend initiation of low-dose estrogen replacement between 11 and 12 years of age if FSH is elevated on at least two sequential measurements, with estrogen dosage increased slowly to adult replacement over 2–4 years .This therapy benefits bone density, uterine health, and cognitive functioning. TD estradiol is preferred over oral forms, as it bypasses hepatic metabolism, reducing stroke and hypertension risks .v Transdermal estradiol is recommended when possible, with oral estradiol as second choice . Progestin should be added after 2 years of estrogen therapy or upon the occurrence of breakthrough bleeding to reduce the risk of endometrial cancer. Guidelines recommend adding cyclic progesterone once breakthrough bleeding occurs (most commonly after ~18–24 months of unopposed estrogen exposure); preferred option is micronized progesterone 200 mg for 10–12 days per month .

Fertility Options

While most women with TS are infertile, assisted reproductive technologies (ART) offer options for family planning. Approximately 5–8% of women with TS experience spontaneous pregnancy, although there is a heightened risk of miscarriage, fetal abnormalities, and pre-eclampsia.v,xxi For women with mosaic TS, oocyte cryopreservation may be considered, and oocyte donation has higher success rates than in vitro fertilization.v Comprehensive cardiac screening is recommended before attempting pregnancy due to increased cardiovascular risks. For patients using their own oocytes, preimplantation testing should be offered; individuals with TS mosaicism who become pregnant spontaneously should be offered prenatal diagnostic testing . To enable timely fertility preservation referral, guidelines recommend measuring LH, FSH, and AMH at 8–9 years and yearly until 11–12 years .

PROGNOSIS

The prognosis of TS varies widely based on karyotype and the presence of associated medical conditions. Individuals with a 45,X karyotype generally exhibit more severe symptoms, including more significant risks of cardiovascular issues and lymphatic abnormalities. Mosaicism, particularly 45,X/46,XX, often corresponds with milder clinical presentations and a higher likelihood of spontaneous pregnancy.v However, correlation between karyotype and phenotype is highly variable .

However, even in cases with milder phenotypes, TS patients are at elevated risk for long-term health issues such as osteoporosis, cardiovascular disease, and metabolic disorders. Monitoring and early intervention are key to improving long-term outcomes.

TS patients generally have average intelligence, although some may experience spatial awareness, attention, and executive functioning challenges. Early psychosocial support and educational accommodations are beneficial, as late diagnoses may be associated with unfavorable psychosocial outcomes.

SUMMARY AND RECOMMENDATIONS

TS is a complex condition that requires comprehensive, lifelong management. Early diagnosis and timely intervention are crucial to address potential health complications and improve quality of life. Recommendations for managing TS include:

Cardiac screening: regular cardiac monitoring with TTE and CMR should begin at diagnosis and continue at intervals specified for children and adults.

Audiometric and renal assessments: hearing and kidney evaluations should be conducted at diagnosis, with periodic follow-ups, especially in patients with known renal anomalies.

Endocrine and growth management: GH therapy should start as early as possible, with individualized dosing to optimize growth outcomes. Estrogen therapy should begin at the onset of puberty and include progestin after 2 years to protect endometrial health.

Fertility counseling and reproductive options: patients should be counseled on reproductive risks, and ART options such as oocyte donation should be discussed, with thorough cardiac screening before pregnancy.

Psychosocial and educational support: support systems and educational accommodations are recommended to promote optimal developmental outcomes.
