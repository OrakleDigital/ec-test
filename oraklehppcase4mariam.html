Introduction

A 14-Year-Old Female with Joint Pain, Fatigue, and Social Withdrawal

History of Present Illness

Welcome. Your patient today is Mariam F.

Mariam is a 14-year-old female presenting with a history of pain and stiffness in the knees and hips, fatigue, muscle weakness, and diminished hearing, which has progressively impacted her ability to engage in physical activities and her daily routines. These conditions have added complexity to her care, affecting her school attendance and participation in extracurricular activities, including her favorite sport, soccer. Once an active and outgoing student, Mariam has gradually withdrawn from activities she once enjoyed, such as hiking with her family and participating in school dance performances, further isolating her from her regular routines.

Emotional and social challenges have accompanied her physical limitations and medical conditions. Her mother reports that she has begun missing school regularly due to fatigue and discomfort. Mariam has withdrawn from social interactions with her friends, avoiding group outings and school events she used to enjoy, such as movie nights and birthday parties. Mariam’s growing frustration with her condition has led to signs of depression and anxiety, including increased irritability, low mood, and a reluctance to talk about her feelings.

Family and Social History

Mariam’s family history reveals no known skeletal or metabolic disorders. Her father, as well as her paternal and maternal grandparents, have experienced varying degrees of chronic conditions, although no formal diagnoses have been reported. Her mother is reportedly healthy with no significant medical conditions. There is no known family history of seizures, hearing loss, or related conditions.

Mariam lives with her parents and has one younger sibling, who is healthy and active. She previously enjoyed hiking with her family, attending school events, and participating in team sports, mainly soccer and dance. However, due to her medical conditions, she has withdrawn from these activities over the past two years. She has missed significant time from school, affecting her academic and social engagement. The family does not own pets. Her parents deny smoking, consume alcohol occasionally, and have no history of illicit drug use. Mariam’s diet is reportedly well-balanced, with her mother ensuring sufficient calcium and vitamin D intake. However, her family expresses concern about her decreased activity level and its impact on her overall well-being.

Physical Examination Notes

The patient is conscious, alert, and well-oriented x3. She appears fatigued but not in acute distress. She is afebrile, anicteric, and not pale. There is no swelling or palpable lymphadenopathy.

Case Review

This case highlights the importance of recognizing critical diagnostic clues and the challenges of diagnosing a rare condition like HPP. Mariam’s clinical history revealed early signs that warranted closer evaluation but were initially missed, delaying appropriate diagnosis. Her persistent fatigue, pain and stiffness in the knees and hips, and lack of focal neurological deficits should have prompted consideration of metabolic disorders.

The consistently low ALP level was a significant biochemical abnormality that differentiated HPP from other seizure-related conditions, such as pyridoxine-dependent epilepsy or mitochondrial disorders. However, this was not initially considered low due to the laboratory routinely applying an adult reference range. Additionally, her elevated vitamin B6 levels were definitive diagnostic guides. These clues, combined with her clinical presentation and absence of a family history of epilepsy, should have guided earlier testing and HPP diagnosis.

Genetic testing subsequently identified ALPL mutations in both the child (c.346G>A [p.Ala116Thr] pathogenic variant) and the mother, confirming the diagnosis.

This case underscores the need for heightened clinician awareness of rare metabolic disorders and their subtle yet critical signs. A systematic approach that integrates clinical history, laboratory findings, and genetic testing is essential for early diagnosis.

Hypophosphatasia (HPP) is a rare genetic metabolic disorder characterized by defects in the tissue non-specific alkaline phosphatase (TNSALP) enzyme. The clinical manifestations of Hypophosphatasia (HPP) differ depending on age and severity, and the condition is commonly categorized into six forms: perinatal (severe and benign), infantile, childhood, adult, and odontohypophosphatasia .

Non-skeletal symptoms across all age groups often include premature loss of primary teeth with intact roots, failure to thrive or feeding difficulties in infancy, hypotonia or muscle weakness, pyridoxine-responsive seizures due to disrupted vitamin B6 metabolism, and generalized fatigue or developmental delays in the absence of obvious skeletal abnormalities . These symptoms frequently lead to referrals to specialists such as neurologists, dentists, or pediatricians, delaying an accurate diagnosis.

In addition to these non-skeletal features, skeletal symptoms are a prominent aspect of HPP and can provide critical diagnostic clues. Infants and young children with HPP may present with delayed closure of fontanelles, bowed or shortened limbs, and deformities such as rachitic-like chest or limb abnormalities caused by defective bone mineralization. Fractures primarily occur in long bones .

Complicating matters, HPP's clinical features can mimic conditions like rickets, epilepsy, or other neuromuscular disorders, making it crucial to identify red flags that point specifically to HPP.

The diagnostic criteria for HPP in children rely on a combination of laboratory findings, clinical indicators, radiological evidence, and, when needed, confirmatory genetic testing . Here are the proposed diagnostic criteria for the diagnosis of hypophosphatasia in children and adolescents: according to results from the HPP International Working Group :

Children

Major Criteria

  • Pathogenic or likely pathogenic ALPL gene variant
  • Elevation of natural substrates (eg plasma vitamin B6 measurement should be done after discontinuation of pyridoxine supplementation for at least one week to avoid falsely elevated levels and ensure accurate assessment )
  • Early nontraumatic loss of primary teeth
  • Presence of rickets on radiographs

Minor Criteria

  • Short stature or linear growth failure over time
  • Delayed motor milestones
  • Craniosynostosis
  • Nephrocalcinosis
  • B6 responsive seizures

Diagnosis: 2 major or 1 major + 2 minor

Diagnosing HPP requires either two major or one major and two minor criteria. The use of low ALP activity as an obligate criterion requires the exclusion of other conditions that can cause low ALP activity and multiple instances of low ALP activity.

ALP and Its Role in the Differential Diagnosis of HPP

Reduced serum alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity is a hallmark of hypophosphatasia (HPP), often accompanied by impaired bone mineralization and rickets-like changes visible on X-ray imaging. The deficiency of tissue non-specific alkaline phosphatase (TNSALP) leads to the accumulation of its substrates, including phosphoethanolamine (PEA), inorganic pyrophosphate, and pyridoxal 5’-phosphate (PLP), which further contribute to the clinical manifestations of the disease .

While low ALP activity is a critical diagnostic clue, interpretation requires comparing age- and sex-specific reference ranges to account for physiological variations . Furthermore, a low ALP level is associated with numerous diseases, such as a nutritional disorder, zinc deficiency, Cushing’s syndrome, and cleidocranial dysplasia. Hence, for a definitive diagnosis of HPP, genetic testing of the ALPL gene is recommended .

Conversely, normal or elevated ALP levels are associated with other skeletal conditions such as rickets or osteomalacia (due to vitamin D deficiency), osteitis deformans (Paget’s disease) , and osteogenesis imperfecta, helping to distinguish these from HPP.

By integrating ALP findings with clinical, biochemical, and radiological data, clinicians can improve the accuracy of diagnosis in patients with HPP.
