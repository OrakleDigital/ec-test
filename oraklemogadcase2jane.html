Introduction

Unraveling the Cause of Relapsing Neurological Symptoms in a 51-Year-Old Female

History of Present Illness

Welcome. Your patient today is Jane T.

Jane, a 51-year-old female, initially presented to her primary care physician eight months ago with complaints of severe fatigue, left-sided facial weakness, and bilateral sensorineural hearing loss. Despite her symptoms, she had no significant medical history and led a relatively active lifestyle, enjoying gardening and community volunteer work. Initial treatment with intravenous steroids for 5 days resulted in significant symptom improvement over several weeks.

However, one month later, she experienced a relapse with worsening neurological symptoms, including leg weakness, right-sided appendicular ataxia, and frequent falls. She also developed increasing difficulty speaking, a tingling sensation in her head and neck, and persistent headaches. These new neurological symptoms prompted a hospital visit, where she was again treated with intravenous steroids, leading to partial symptom resolution.

Over the next several months, Jane continued to experience relapsing episodes characterized by fluctuating neurological deficits, including recurrent visual disturbances, ataxia, and limb weakness. Each episode showed a pattern of initial steroid responsiveness followed by symptom recurrence.

Based on her clinical presentation and MRI findings, she was diagnosed with chronic lymphocytic inflammation with pontine perivascular enhancement responsive to steroids (CLIPPERS). She was treated with a short course of intravenous corticosteroids, followed by oral prednisone for maintenance therapy.

Approximately 1 month ago, Jane’s condition deteriorated when she began tapering the corticosteroids, prompting her to visit the hospital. She reported progressive leg weakness, right-sided appendicular ataxia, and frequent falls. Additionally, she experienced increasing difficulty speaking, a tingling sensation in her head and neck, and persistent headaches. These symptoms, despite ongoing immunosuppressive treatment and relapse upon taper, raised concerns about whether the CLIPPERS diagnosis fully explained her condition.

Routine hematology, biochemistry and metabolic panel results were within normal limits, ruling out systemic metabolic abnormalities as a contributing factor.

She presents to you for further evaluation in an attempt to clarify the diagnosis.

Family and Social History

No known family history of autoimmune or demyelinating diseases. She denies tobacco use, drinks alcohol socially, and has no history of recreational drug use. No known environmental exposures or infections in her recent history could explain her symptoms.

Review of Systems

General: severe fatigue.

Neurological: left-sided facial weakness, progressive leg weakness, right-sided incoordination, frequent falls, slurred speech, persistent headaches, and tingling in the head and neck.

Ophthalmologic: bilateral blurry vision.

Physical Examination Notes

Jane is alert but fatigued, with left-sided facial weakness, slurred speech, lower extremity weakness (3/5), and right-sided ataxia. Sensory deficits in the head and neck and brisk reflexes with bilateral Babinski signs are noted. Gait is severely unstable, limiting mobility.

Case Review

Jane, a 51-year-old female, initially presented to her primary care physician with complaints of severe fatigue, left-sided facial weakness, and bilateral blurry vision. Despite no significant medical history, she led a relatively active lifestyle, enjoying gardening and community volunteer work. Initial treatment with intravenous steroids for 5 days resulted in significant symptom improvement, and based on her clinical presentation and MRI findings, she was diagnosed with CLIPPERS and started on ongoing corticosteroid therapy for disease maintenance.

However, despite being on chronic steroid treatment, Jane experienced a relapse one month later, with progressive leg weakness, right-sided appendicular ataxia, and frequent falls. She also developed increasing difficulty speaking, a tingling sensation in her head and neck, and persistent headaches. These new symptoms prompted a hospital visit, where she received another course of intravenous steroids, leading to partial symptom resolution.

Over the following months, Jane continued to experience relapsing episodes, characterized by fluctuating neurological deficits, including recurrent visual disturbances, ataxia, and progressive limb weakness. Each episode followed a pattern of initial response to steroids, followed by symptom recurrence despite ongoing corticosteroid therapy. The persistence of these relapses raised concerns that the initial CLIPPERS diagnosis did not fully explain her condition, prompting further evaluation.

Subsequent investigations, including repeated neuroimaging, cerebrospinal fluid analysis, and serum antibody testing, ultimately led to the diagnosis of MOGAD. Her relapsing course, MRI findings, and positive MOG-IgG antibody confirmed the diagnosis, highlighting the importance of reassessing patients with ongoing disease activity despite treatment.

Key Diagnostic Clues from Her History and Presentation

  • Childhood neurological event: her history of a multifocal neurological episode, characterized by fever, dysarthria, bilateral vision loss, and unilateral weakness, followed by resolution with steroids, points to an inflammatory demyelinating process. This relapsing pattern is consistent with MOGAD and not typical of static conditions like CLIPPERS or granulomatous diseases like sarcoidosis.
  • Sensorineural hearing loss: the unexplained development of bilateral hearing loss highlights systemic neurological involvement, which can occur in MOGAD, because MOGAD can cause polyfocal demyelinating brain lesions that involve key structures related to auditory processing .
  • Current presentation: Jane’s current symptoms of bilateral blurry vision, facial weakness, progressive leg weakness, appendicular ataxia, dysarthria, and multiple falls. The lack of symptoms confined to the pons or cerebellum (typical of CLIPPERS) helped exclude this alternative diagnosis .
  • Imaging findings: her brain MRI revealed multifocal supra- and infratentorial demyelinating lesions, including brainstem involvement, without the characteristic symmetric perivascular pontine enhancement seen in CLIPPERS . The multifocal nature aligns more closely with MOGAD.
  • CSF and serum studies: CSF and serum studies in Jane’s case further support the diagnosis of MOGAD. The absence of oligoclonal bands and a high IgG index in the CSF ruled out multiple sclerosis (MS). Additionally, negative infectious and vasculitis panels excluded alternative inflammatory or infectious causes. Most critically, Jane's MOG-IgG titre of 1:100 was a clear positive, reinforcing the diagnosis. Jane's combination of brainstem involvement, characteristic MRI findings, lesion resolution over time, and negative AQP4-IgG testing strongly supports MOGAD as the correct diagnosis .
  • Relapses despite ongoing corticosteroid therapy: Jane’s relapsing course despite ongoing corticosteroid therapy further suggested that her initial diagnosis of CLIPPERS was incorrect. CLIPPERS is a chronic inflammatory condition that typically remains steroid-responsive with stable disease control on maintenance therapy. However, Jane’s recurrent relapses, with initial steroid responsiveness followed by symptom recurrence, were atypical for CLIPPERS and more consistent with a relapsing demyelinating disorder such as MOGAD. This pattern of steroid-dependent but relapsing disease progression was a critical clue prompting further evaluation, ultimately leading to the correct diagnosis.

MOGAD is a recently recognized inflammatory demyelinating condition of the CNS. Discovered in 2007, its epidemiology remains incompletely understood due to the delayed availability of widespread diagnostic testing .

Studies suggest an incidence of 1.6-3.4 per million person-years in Europe. Unlike AQP4-IgG NMOSD, MOGAD shows no significant sex disparity, and has a median onset age of 20-30 years, but it can affect persons of all ages .

Geographic variations are notable, with MOGAD being more frequent than AQP4-IgG NMOSD in some regions, such as the UK and Sri Lanka, but less common in Korea. No high-risk ethnicities have been identified .

Burden of Disease in MOGAD

MOGAD imposes a significant burden on patients due to its unpredictable course and risk of long-term neurological disability. While some patients experience a monophasic episode, others develop a relapsing disease, with the likelihood of relapse increasing with longer follow-up—affecting up to 70% of patients, including both children and adults with a median onset age of 26 years , recurrent attacks can lead to accumulating disability .

Beyond physical disability, MOGAD imposes a substantial psychosocial burden. Many patients experience anxiety, depression, and emotional distress. While receiving a definitive diagnosis often alleviates some emotional distress, a subset of patients report worsened mental health outcomes. The long-term consequences of diagnostic delays—reported by patients to include vision impairment, neurological complications, and bladder dysfunction—further exacerbate the disease burden .

Delays in MOGAD diagnosis, often due to symptom overlap with CNS demyelinating diseases and lack of awareness, lead to misdiagnosis, inappropriate treatments, and prolonged disease progression . Some patients delay seeking care, while others face dismissal of symptoms , particularly women, who are more likely to be misdiagnosed with mental health conditions. Limited MOG-Ig testing contributes to these challenges . Improved education for healthcare providers, earlier diagnosis, and targeted treatment strategies are essential to reducing the burden of MOGAD .

Unmet Medical Need

Myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody-associated disease (MOGAD) remains a rare and underrecognized demyelinating disorder with significant unmet medical needs. Unlike multiple sclerosis (MS) and neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD), which have well-established treatment guidelines and FDA-approved therapies, there are currently no FDA-approved treatments for MOGAD. Management relies on off-label use of immunosuppressive agents such as corticosteroids, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), rituximab, and mycophenolate mofetil, none of which are specifically tailored to the disease’s unique pathophysiology.

The lack of approved therapies creates challenges in standardizing treatment strategies, leading to variability in care and potential delays in optimal management. Patients with MOGAD often experience relapses despite initial treatment, requiring ongoing immunosuppression to prevent further neurological disability. However, the long-term safety and efficacy of these therapies in MOGAD remain unclear, as most data are extrapolated from MS or NMOSD studies rather than MOGAD-specific research.

Furthermore, delays in diagnosis contribute to inadequate treatment initiation, increasing the risk of unnecessary disability. The fluctuating disease course, overlapping symptoms with other demyelinating disorders, and the need for specialized antibody testing often lead to misdiagnosis or delayed recognition. Improved awareness, better diagnostic tools, and targeted therapeutic development are essential to addressing this growing unmet need and ensuring MOGAD patients receive appropriate, evidence-based care.

Pathophysiology of MOGAD

MOGAD pathophysiology is not fully understood but is likely driven by autoantibodies targeting MOG, a highly immunogenic protein on the outermost layer of the myelin sheath. These antibodies have been shown to directly induce demyelination and complement deposition in experimental models, although most human MOG antibodies do not recognize rodent MOG, limiting direct application of animal research .

Pathological features of MOGAD include perivenous and cortical demyelination, CD4-positive T-cell and granulocytic inflammation, and complement activation, distinguishing it from MS, which features CD8-positive inflammation. Unlike NMOSD, AQP4 expression remains intact, and astrocytic damage is minimal. These unique features emphasize MOGAD’s distinct mechanisms of immune-mediated myelin damage, warranting further investigation into its pathogenesis and clinical implications .

Clinical Presentation of MOGAD

MOGAD commonly presents with optic neuritis in adults and ADEM in children under 11 years old. Transverse myelitis is also frequent, while less common manifestations include cerebral cortical encephalitis with seizures, brainstem or cerebellar demyelinating attacks, tumefactive brain lesions, and progressive white matter damage .

Unlike AQP4-IgG NMOSD and MS, MOGAD lacks significant sex or racial predominance and can present as either a monophasic or relapsing disease. This variability underscores its distinct clinical features and the need for individualized diagnostic and treatment strategies .

Diagnosis in MOGAD

MOGAD has been underdiagnosed due to the recent discovery of MOG-IgG in 2007 and the delayed availability of widespread testing. Early reports likely underestimated its frequency, highlighting the need for improved diagnostic awareness and tools to better understand its true prevalence .

The method used to detect MOG-IgG antibodies is important for accurate diagnosis, as different assays vary in sensitivity and specificity . Live cell-based assays (CBAs) are considered the gold standard for MOG-IgG detection due to their superior sensitivity and ability to detect pathogenic antibodies more reliably than fixed CBAs . This distinction is especially important in cases where low-positive titers are observed, as fixed CBAs may fail to detect MOG-IgG in some patients who genuinely have MOGAD. Given these limitations, live CBAs should be prioritized whenever possible for MOG-IgG testing to ensure accurate diagnosis and reduce the risk of misclassification .

Diagnostic Criteria for MOGAD

The diagnosis of MOGAD requires the fulfillment of the following 3 criteria :

(1) Core Clinical Demyelinating Event

At least 1 of the following clinical presentations:

  • Optic neuritis
  • Myelitis
  • ADEM
  • Cerebral monofocal or polyfocal deficits
  • Brainstem or cerebellar deficits
  • Cerebral cortical encephalitis, often associated with seizures

Less common presentations include cerebral cortical encephalitis (often with seizures), brainstem and cerebellar demyelinating attacks, tumefactive brain lesions, cerebral monofocal and polyfocal CNS deficits associated with demyelinating lesions, cranial neuropathies, and progressive white matter damage (leukodystrophy-like pattern).

(2) Positive MOG-IgG Test

Detection of MOG-IgG via a cell-based serum assay, with the following interpretations:

  • Clear positive: no additional supporting features required.
  • Low positive: requires:
    • seronegativity for AQP4-IgG, and
    • at least one supporting clinical or MRI feature.
  • Positive without reported titer: seronegativity for AQP4-IgG, and at least 1 supporting feature needed.
  • Negative serum but positive CSF: seronegativity for AQP4-IgG, and at least 1 supporting feature required.

Supporting Clinical or MRI Features

  • Optic Neuritis
    • Bilateral simultaneous clinical involvement
    • Longitudinal optic nerve involvement (>50% length of the nerve)
    • Perineural optic sheath enhancement
    • Optic disc edema
  • Myelitis
    • Longitudinally extensive myelitis
    • Central cord lesion or H-sign
    • Conus lesion
  • Brain, Brainstem, or Cerebral Syndrome
    • Multiple ill-defined T2 hyperintense lesions in the supratentorial and infratentorial white matter
    • Deep gray matter involvement
    • Ill-defined T2 hyperintensities in the pons, middle cerebellar peduncle, or medulla
    • Cortical lesions, with or without lesional and overlying meningeal enhancement

The Timing of MOG-IgG Testing is Important

The timing of MOG-IgG antibody testing plays a pivotal role in accurate diagnosis. Research has demonstrated that low-positive MOG-IgG results are more commonly associated with a longer time from disease onset to sampling . MOG-IgG titers tend to decline over time, meaning the timing of the assay can significantly impact diagnostic accuracy.

In a cohort of MOG-IgG antibody-positive patients tested within 6 months of onset, antibody titers were shown to decline progressively. By 6 months, approximately 25% of patients returned a low-positive result, and by 12 months, 50% returned a negative result. This finding highlights the potential for missed or unclear diagnoses if testing occurs too long after disease onset .

Early testing during or shortly after a clinical event is critical to maximize diagnostic sensitivity and ensure timely, accurate identification of MOGAD.
