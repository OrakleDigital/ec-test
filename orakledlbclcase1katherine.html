Welcome. Your patient today is Katherine L.

Katherine is a 63-year-old woman who presented to her primary care physician with worsening fatigue over the previous two months.

She mentions that she was usually very active, attending Zumba classes three times a week, but her energy levels had significantly declined over the past two months, and she started missing classes. Katherine works as a financial adviser in an office, but she has been working remotely from home for the past two weeks, as she found getting out of the house to be too strenuous.

Case Review

Estimates indicate 11,400 people in Canada will be diagnosed with NHL in 2022 . DLBCL is the most frequent NHL, accounting for about 30% of cases diagnosed annually (Padala & Kallam, 2022).

DLBCL not otherwise specified (NOS) (as per World Health Organization classification [Alaggio et al., 2022]) accounts for more than 80% of large B-cell lymphoma entities (Sehn & Salles, 2021). Morphologically, lymph nodes abnormally present with sheets of large cells with enlarged nuclei, basophilic cytoplasm, high proliferation rate, and expression of pan-B cell antigens (e.g., CD20 and CD79a), distributed in a diffuse (rather than grouped) pattern (Padala & Kallam, 2022; Susanibar-Adaniya & Barta, 2021).

Classification

DLBCL NOS results from the malignant proliferation of B-cells during their various stages of development (Padala & Kallam, 2022), and can be further classified into several morphological, molecular, and immunohistochemical subgroups .

DLBCL is most frequently diagnosed in older people, with a median age at presentation of 65–70 years (Sehn & Salles, 2021). It typically presents as a rapidly growing mass or enlarging lymph nodes in a nodal or extranodal site (Padala & Kallam, 2022). Primary nodal presentation is the most common (30–40%), although 50% of patients also have organ (extranodal) involvement at diagnosis, usually affecting the gastrointestinal tract, bone marrow, and skin. (Lymphoma Canada, 2021; Padala & Kallam, 2021). Approximately 30% of the patients show “B symptoms, " including fever, night sweats, and weight loss (Padala & Kallam, 2022). Therefore, the initial diagnostic work-up should include a detailed clinical and physical examination, including a whole-body lymph node survey and an abdominal examination to evaluate for enlarged liver and spleen (Padala & Kallam, 2022).

In addition to morphologic characteristics, specialized tests including IHC and/or flow cytometry, FISH, and molecular testing are needed to accurately diagnose DLBCL (Sehn & Salles, 2021). GEP is preferably used to determine COO as the gold-standard method; in clinical practice, however, GEP has not been widely adopted as most GEP technologies require fresh-frozen tumor tissue . Therefore, IHC algorithms have been developed as substitutes and applied with varying concordance to GEP – with an overall concordance of 72% in a study of 359 homogeneously treated patients with DLBCL .

The classification of DLBCL by COO is a recognized gold-standard approach for the molecular classification of DLBCL and provides valuable prognostic information . Two main genetic COO-based subtypes are widely recognized: GCB subtype (~60% of cases) and ABC subtype (20–30%), which derive from cells of GC exit or post-GC origin, with either germinal center-exit or early plasmablastic phenotype (Alaggio et al., 2022; Padala & Kallam, 2022). GCB has a GEP related to GC B-cells, including BCL6 and EZH2. In contrast, the ABC subtype is characterized by dependence on B-cell receptor (BCR) signaling and nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of ABC (NF-κB), being negative for most GC markers, and expressing IRF4/MUM1 (Alaggio et al., 2022; Sehn & Salles, 2021). A third group of unclassified DLBCL (10–15%) which includes cases not expressing either set of genes characteristic of GCB or ABC subgroups . These genetic subtypes are associated with prognostic differences in clinical outcomes, with GCB associated with significantly higher survival than the other subtypes (Nowakowski & Czuczman, 2015; Papageorgiou et al., 2021).

Given the limited capacity to routinely perform GEP on fresh frozen samples, IHC algorithms providing an approximation of GEP have been commonly used instead to determine COO in clinical practice (Sehn & Salles, 2021; Susanibar-Adaniya & Barta, 2021). However, this method does not recognize the “unclassified” GEP category, so a GCB/non-GCB distinction is made instead . The Hans algorithm (Table 1) is the most widely utilized, covering CD10, BCL6, and MUM1 .

Further molecular heterogeneity, including recurrent genetic rearrangements associated with poorer outcomes (MYC, BCL2, BCL6), may be detected by FISH and can be used to genetically define subtypes beyond the COO (Sehn & Salles, 2021). A MYC rearrangement is seen in 12% of cases. In contrast, a MYC rearrangement concurrent with a rearrangement in BCL2, BCL6, or both occurs in 4–8% of cases with morphologic features of DLBCL, the majority of which are the GCB subtype, in which BCL2 rearrangements occur exclusively (Sehn & Salles, 2021). These cases are now classified as “high-grade B-cell lymphoma with MYC and BCL2 and/or BCL6 rearrangements”, commonly referred to as double- or triple-hit lymphoma (Sehn & Salles, 2021).

Staging

To guide management, patients with DLBCL need a complete staging work-up (Sehn & Salles, 2021). This requires establishing the patient’s performance status and detailed laboratory testing, including a complete blood count (CBC) with differential, liver function tests, LDH, and creatinine (Padala & Kallam, 2022; Sehn & Salles, 2021). LDH, a predictor of survival, is elevated in >50% of cases (Padala & Kallam, 2022). Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) testing should be considered as B-cell lymphomas can be an acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS)-defining illness (Padala & Kallam, 2022). Hepatitis B and C virus (HBV/HCV) testing should also be performed, since DLBCL treatment involves significant immunosuppression (Padala & Kallam, 2022).

Imaging with PET/CT may be required for staging (Sehn & Salles, 2021). To rule out occult central nervous system (CNS) involvement, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain and cytologic evaluation of cerebrospinal fluid should be conducted in patients at high risk of CNS involvement (Sehn & Salles, 2021). An echocardiogram or multi-gated acquisition scan is required if an anthracycline-based regimen is planned (Spinner & Advani, 2022).

In addition to routine laboratory evaluation and performance status assessment, baseline cardiac assessment should include an electrocardiogram, with echocardiography considered for all patients and recommended in older patients or those with cardiovascular risk factors . Positron emission tomography–computed tomography is recommended as the standard imaging modality for baseline staging and response assessment, and may also be considered at interim assessment . Central nervous system assessment with contrast-enhanced cranial magnetic resonance imaging and cerebrospinal fluid evaluation may be performed on a case-by-case basis according to clinical risk factors or presentation . Fluorescence in situ hybridization testing should be performed to determine the presence of MYC rearrangements; if MYC is rearranged, fluorescence in situ hybridization assessment for BCL2 rearrangement is recommended .

Some of these investigations can be integrated to calculate the IPI based on the patient’s age, Ann Arbor stage, serum LDH level, performance status, and number of extranodal sites, or its derived scores – the revised IPI (R-IPI) and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN)-IPI (Sehn & Salles, 2021; Spinner & Advani, 2022). A CNS-IPI, consisting of common IPI variables plus renal or adrenal involvement, may be used to identify patients at high risk of CNS involvement (Sehn & Salles, 2021; Shafey et al., 2021). These prognostic indexes stratify patients into risk groups (Table 2) (NCCN, 2023; Sehn & Salles, 2021; Spinner & Advani, 2022).

Investigations are important to distinguish patients with limited-stage from those with advanced-stage disease (Sehn & Salles, 2021). Notably, approximately 70% of patients present with advanced-stage DLBCL (Padala & Kallam, 2022; Sehn & Salles, 2021). IPI holds more value in limited-stage disease, and clinical indexes should not be used to identify patients at very high risk or to discern biological heterogeneity (Sehn & Salles, 2021). Therefore, the presence of other prognostic features such as biologic-associated factors (e.g., COO) (Sehn & Salles, 2021) should also be considered, since they may provide valuable prognostic information independently of IPI (Papageorgiou et al., 2021; Spinner & Advani, 2022).

Limited stage is defined by the 2021 Lymphoma Canada evidence-based guideline for DLBCL as IPI 0–1, no bulky disease, and ≤1 extranodal site . Bulky disease has no unique definition, although most provinces specify bulk as >7–10 cm . Advanced stage includes stage III/IV disease and may include patients with B-symptoms and bulky disease . Existing guidelines defined high-risk advanced-stage disease as an IPI ≥3 .

There remains debate about whether the delineation between limited and advanced stage reflects earlier identification of a disease or a biologically distinct entity with different risks and outcome features (Rojek & Smith, 2022).

Management

In the 1990s, CHOP was the standard-of-care for DLBCL. However, the clinical benefit of adding rituximab was later demonstrated in several clinical trials, becoming the new standard first-line treatment (Candelaria & Dueñas-Gonzalez, 2021). Overall, R-CHOP cures about 70% of patients, and patients with localized disease have an overall survival (OS) of up to 92% (Candelaria & Dueñas-Gonzalez, 2021).

The number of treatment cycles of R-CHOP and the role of radiotherapy may differ based on the stage of disease and bulk. According to the 2021 Lymphoma Canada evidence-based guideline for the treatment of DLBCL, for patients who test negative for molecular features, the treatment strategy is based on limited versus advanced-stage disease . Thus, Katherine’s treatment strategy should be based on the recommendations for advanced-stage DLBCL. Other factors that may affect treatment choices are CNS involvement and cardiac toxicity , lacking in Katherine’s case.

The recommended frontline therapy for patients with advanced-stage DLBCL consists of R-CHOP x 6, given every 21 days, as recommended by all provinces (Table 3) . The results of multiple randomized trials and population-based studies have demonstrated no added benefit with 8 versus six cycles (Spinner & Advani, 2022). Other intensification strategies have been proposed, such as increasing dose density with 14-day versus 21-day cycles; however, this also failed to improve outcomes while increasing toxicity and compromising dose delivery, particularly in patients aged ≥60 years (Candelaria & Dueñas-Gonzalez, 2021; Spinner & Advani, 2022)

Abbreviated therapy (3–4 cycles) is only recommended for patients with limited-stage disease . Options for these patients include treatment with R-CHOP x 4 + 2 doses of rituximab, or a planned combined modality with R-CHOP x 3 + ISRT, or PET-guided therapy, which have been used in certain Canadian centers/provinces for limited-stage disease with zero to low risk factors (Table 4) .

Recognizing the biologic heterogeneity of DLBCL, several trials over the last decade explored whether the addition of novel agents onto an R-CHOP backbone could improve outcomes on preselected (or retrospectively analyzed) patients with ABC (or non-GCB) subtype (Spinner & Advani, 2022). These included bortezomib, ibrutinib, and lenalidomide, all of which had displayed preferential activity in ABC DLBCL in the relapsed/refractory (R/R) setting, but failed to demonstrated benefit over R-CHOP in terms of progression-free survival (PFS) or OS in Phase 3 trials, with the additional risk of higher toxicity and limited benefit in older patients (Table 5) (Ngu et al., 2022; Spinner & Advani, 2022). DA-EPOCH-R also failed to demonstrate superiority to R-CHOP and was associated with greater hematologic toxicity .

The replacement of rituximab by obinutuzumab, an anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody engineered to offer greater direct cell death and more potent antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity and phagocytosis, did not show superiority to rituximab (Table 5) (Ngu et al., 2022; Spinner & Advani, 2022).

In 2022, the POLARIX trial demonstrated that adding anti-CD79b antibody-drug conjugate polatuzumab vedotin to rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, and prednisone (pola-R-CHP) significantly improved PFS compared to R-CHOP in patients with previously untreated intermediate-risk or high-risk DLBCL (Table 5) . The overall safety profile was generally similar in the pola-R-CHP and R-CHOP groups, with mostly similar types and incidences of adverse events of both any grade and grade 3 or 4 reported in the two groups

Additionally, PFS data after a median follow-up of 3 years continued to show a statistically significant reduction in the risk of disease worsening or death with pola-R-CHP compared with R-CHOP (hazard ratio [HR], 0.76; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.60–0.97) . After a median follow-up of 39.7 months, OS data were immature with few events in each arm and remained similar between pola-R-CHP and R-CHOP (HR, 0.94; 95% CI, 0.67–1.33; P=0.73) .

Pola-R-CHP has become a first-line option in eligible patients in the United States (NCCN category 1 recommendation) and Europe . In combination with R-CHP, Polatuzumab vedotin has been approved by Health Canada for the treatment of adult patients with previously untreated LBCL, including DLBCL NOS . In view of its recent approval, polatuzumab vedotin was not included in the 2021 edition of the Canadian evidence-based guideline for the frontline treatment of DLBCL .

Polatuzumab vedotin in combination with bendamustine and rituximab was already approved in Canada for the treatment of adults with R/R DLBCL NOS, not eligible for autologous stem cell transplant (ASCT), who have received ≥1 prior therapy , and its use in this setting is discussed in the Canadian guidelines for the treatment of R/R DLBCL .

There is no Canadian consensus for the optimal follow-up care for asymptomatic survivors of lymphoma who have received treatment with curative intent .

Following R-CHOP, response can be assessed with PET to detect nonresponding or progressive disease according to Lugano criteria, with interpretation according to the Deauville 5-point scale (Sehn & Salles, 2021; Shafey et al., 2021). A Deauville score ≤3 is generally considered equivalent to CR . These patients should be observed without further treatment . Since maintenance therapy has not shown value in this setting . Rituximab, enzastaurin, and everolimus maintenance failed to prolong PFS in this patient group (Spinner & Advani, 2022). In a Phase 3 study, lenalidomide maintenance showed increased PFS in older patients (HR, 0.71; 95% CI, 0.54–0.93; P=0.01), suggesting a potential role in older adults with DLBCL . However, no significant difference was seen in patients with a non-GCB profile, such as Katherine, and it was associated with higher toxicity than placebo, leading to premature discontinuation in 36% of patients . However, if patients show focal PET positivity (Deauville 4–5) at the end of R-CHOP treatment, local consolidative ISRT may be considered .

ASCT is usually recommended for chemotherapy-sensitive R/R disease, rather than immediately following CR1 (Sehn & Salles, 2021). Exceptionally, some Canadian provinces may consider ASCT in patients aged under 70 years with 4–5 high-risk factors, especially if they are PET-positive after four cycles of R-CHOP .

Routine post-treatment surveillance imaging has not been shown to affect the outcome and is generally discouraged (Melchardt et al., 2023; Sehn & Salles, 2021). Nevertheless, patients should be clinically monitored to detect recurrence, including thorough physical examination and appropriate blood tests (Sehn & Salles, 2021; Sussman et al., 2016). Most studies reported performing clinical follow-up every 2–3 months for the first two years, and then every 4–6 months in the subsequent three years (i.e., years 3–5), with annual visits thereafter, while imaging was performed for patients in whom relapse was suspected . In clinical practice, monitoring has been proposed every three months for two years, and every 6–12 months thereafter (Sehn & Salles, 2021). Consistent with ESMO recommendations, routine surveillance with positron emission tomography–computed tomography or diagnostic computed tomography is generally not recommended for patients in complete remission; follow-up can be discontinued after 3 years in non-transplanted asymptomatic patients who remain in complete remission .
