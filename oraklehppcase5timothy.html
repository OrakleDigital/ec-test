Introduction

A Late Preterm Male Infant with Severe Respiratory Distress, Skeletal Abnormalities, and Hypotonia

History of Present Illness

Welcome. Your patient today is Timothy J.

Timothy, a male infant born at 37 weeks of gestation via cesarean delivery due to prenatal findings of shortened long bones and generalized hypomineralization, presented with severe respiratory distress immediately after birth. The newborn required intubation and mechanical ventilation in the delivery room due to thoracic and pulmonary hypoplasia. On physical examination, he was noted to have shortened and bowed limbs, widely open fontanelles, a short neck, and poor muscle tone. Radiological imaging revealed significant skeletal abnormalities, including generalized hypomineralization and deformities of the long bones. Laboratory studies identified abnormal calcium and phosphate metabolism and elevated vitamin B6 levels.

Family and Social History

The infant’s mother had a history of 3 previous miscarriages with her current partner and 1 healthy child from a previous relationship. The family had no reported history of skeletal disorders, metabolic conditions, or consanguinity.

Case Review

This case highlights the importance of recognizing key diagnostic clues and the challenges of diagnosing a rare condition like HPP. The infant’s clinical presentation, including severe respiratory distress, skeletal abnormalities, and undetectable ALP levels, provided critical evidence for early consideration of a metabolic bone disorder. Initial findings of hypomineralization on imaging and elevated vitamin B6 levels further differentiated HPP from other skeletal or metabolic conditions, such as OI or achondrogenesis.

Family history revealed muscle weakness and bone pain in several paternal relatives, which should have raised suspicion for an inherited condition such as HPP.

Genetic testing subsequently identified pathogenic ALPL mutations in both the infant (c.473-2G>C and c.413G>C variants) and the mother, confirming the diagnosis and illustrating the inherited nature of HPP.

Given the clinical presentation, ordering this genetic test was appropriate, as it provides confirmation of the metabolic basis of the symptoms. However, genetic testing is not required for diagnosis. Early recognition based on clinical, biochemical, and imaging findings supports accurate diagnostic assessment, even before genetic confirmation.

The diagnosis of HPP relies on a systematic approach, integrating clinical, radiological, and laboratory findings. Reduced ALP activity, adjusted for age and sex reference ranges (Khan AA et al, 2024; Rush et al., 2024; ), is a key indicator, with genetic testing confirming the definitive diagnosis . The family’s history of muscle and bone issues underscores the importance of thorough family history-taking in rare genetic conditions.

The infantile form of HPP is associated with a high mortality rate . This condition is characterized by severe complications, which can manifest early in life, with thoracic hypoplasia and resultant respiratory failure being the leading causes of death in neonatal and perinatal cases .

Historical data highlight the gravity of infantile HPP, with nearly 50% of affected infants succumbing to the condition during infancy'. These statistics underscore the critical importance of early diagnosis in HPP, especially in this population.

Fast Facts

  • Incidence rates: the overall incidence of HPP varies geographically, with severe perinatal and infantile forms estimated to occur in as many as 1 in 100,000 globally and up to 1 in 2,500 in the population with a founder effect identified .
  • Initial findings from a longitudinal, prospective, global registry showed children (n=90) experienced an average diagnostic delay of about 13 months, while adults (n=52) experienced a delay of approximately 10 years .

Differential Diagnosis of HPP in a Newborn

The differential diagnosis of hypophosphatasia (HPP) in newborns includes a variety of conditions that present overlapping clinical and biochemical features. Key differentials include :

  • Rickets – Typically associated with vitamin D deficiency or genetic forms of rickets, characterized by low bone mineralization and deformities.
  • Osteomalacia – A disorder of bone softening due to defective mineralization, often related to calcium or phosphate metabolism issues.
  • Osteogenesis Imperfecta – A genetic disorder characterized by brittle bones, frequent fractures, and other connective tissue abnormalities.
  • Nutritional Disorders – Conditions like malnutrition or specific deficiencies (e.g., vitamin D, calcium) can mimic HPP.
  • Zinc Deficiency – Low zinc levels can cause biochemical abnormalities, including low ALP, that resemble HPP.
  • Cushing’s Syndrome – An endocrine disorder that may result in decreased bone density and altered ALP levels.
  • Cleidocranial Dysplasia – A genetic disorder affecting bone development that shares some radiological similarities with HPP.

Biochemical findings, such as persistently low serum alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity confirmed by multiple measurements, are critical for distinguishing HPP. Additional tests include measuring substrates like urinary phosphoethanolamine (PEA) and blood pyridoxal phosphate (PLP), which accumulate in HPP. ALPL gene testing can confirm the diagnosis and help differentiate HPP from other conditions with similar presentations. .

However, age and sex are factors that must be considered in biochemical measurements . Hence, defining age- and sex-specific reference intervals, as shown by the CALIPER study, improves diagnostic accuracy for pediatric metabolic disorders like hypophosphatasia (HPP), ensuring reliable clinical decisions​ .
