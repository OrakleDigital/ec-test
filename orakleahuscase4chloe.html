Severe Acute Kidney Injury and Thrombotic Microangiopathy with Pulmonary Edema in a Previously Healthy Adolescent

History of Present Illness

Welcome. Your patient today is Chloe B.

A 15-year-old previously healthy female presented two and a half weeks ago at a local care center with severe acute kidney injury (AKI), acute pulmonary edema, and respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation. Symptoms began with decreased urine output, vomiting, malaise, shortness of breath, and general unwellness. She was diagnosed with thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) based on elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), bilirubin, creatinine, uremia, hyperkalemia, and thrombocytopenia, with normal ADAMTS13 levels. Laboratory findings also revealed schistocytes on the peripheral smear and undetectable haptoglobin. Stool cultures showed no evidence of Escherichia coli infection. Chest X-ray confirmed severe pulmonary edema.

She underwent plasmapheresis (3–4 sessions) with no clinical improvement and spent several weeks in the initial treatment center. Infectious complications, including sepsis, complicated her course.

A patient was referred to you today. Upon arrival, she underwent a hemodialysis session. She had not received the meningococcal vaccine. Laboratory findings upon arrival included low complements of C3 and C4.

Case Review

The patient was diagnosed with aHUS after ruling out ADAMTS13 deficiency, SLE (via antibody testing), vasculitis (via ANCA), and STEC-HUS. The diagnosis was based on the presence of TMA and the exclusion of other causes. Genetic analysis was conducted to assess factor B, factor H, and factor H antibodies, but treatment was initiated without waiting for the results. The results, which became available later, identified a high-risk mutation. This mutation significantly increases the likelihood of disease relapse, emphasizing the importance of long-term (perhaps lifelong) and closely monitored treatment to optimize patient outcomes.

The faculty author of today’s case opted to use Ravulizumab for this patient, given the need for sustained complement inhibition to manage aHUS while minimizing the treatment burden with extended dosing intervals.

Introduction

Atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (aHUS) is a rare thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) characterized by thrombocytopenia, microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, and kidney damage, often driven by complement dysregulation. Its annual incidence is estimated at 2 cases per million, with mutations in complement-regulating genes (CFH, CFHR3, MCP, etc.) implicated in 40–60% of cases. Rarely, non-complement-mediated causes, such as DGKE deficiency, are observed .

Without treatment, aHUS has a poor prognosis, with rapid progression to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) or death, affecting up to 46% of adults within one month of onset. Complement inhibitor therapies have significantly improved outcomes, though renal complications remain a concern, emphasizing the need for early diagnosis and intervention .

Differential Diagnoses (TTP, STEC-HUS, Vasculitis, Autoimmune Triggers)

Complement-mediated aHUS must be distinguished from other forms of HUS and TTP, especially due to overlapping clinical features like thrombocytopenia and microangiopathic hemolytic anemia. In young children, hereditary complement-HUS, pneumococcal HUS, and rare conditions like congenital TTP are key considerations. Post-diarrheal STEC-HUS is the most common cause in children aged six months to five years, while complement-HUS is more frequent in older children and adolescents (Loirat F & Frémeaux-Bacchi V. 2011).

In adults, aHUS can be triggered by malignancy, medical treatments, autoimmune diseases such as lupus or antiphospholipid syndrome, while pregnancy can trigger both aHUS and TTP at different stages. Neurological symptoms are more prominent in TTP, whereas renal involvement is the hallmark of aHUS. Complement-HUS should always be considered when ADAMTS13 deficiency is absent in patients with TTP-like presentations. Accurate differentiation is essential for timely and targeted treatment (Loirat F & Frémeaux-Bacchi V. 2011; Ávila A, et al. 2023 ).

Role of Complement Inhibitors (including Ravulizumab) in Management

Complement inhibitors, such as eculizumab and ravulizumab, are the cornerstone of aHUS management, effectively reducing the need for dialysis and intensive care admission by targeting complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) .

Ravulizumab offers several advantages, including less frequent dosing (every 4 or 8 weeks *depending on body weight) compared to eculizumab, improving treatment adherence, and reducing the burden on patients and caregivers . Early initiation of complement inhibitors is critical to preventing irreversible kidney damage and other organ complications (Ávila A, et al. 2023). The therapy requires prior meningococcal vaccination or prophylaxis to mitigate the risk of infection, a known side effect of complement blockade. While haematological parameters improve rapidly with C5 inhibition, improvement in renal outcomes is time-dependent – in some patients, renal function continues to improve for up to 2 years after treatment initiation .

Delayed Genetic Testing Results

Genetic testing is critical for guiding long-term treatment strategies , including the duration of complement inhibitor therapy and assessing relapse risk.

However, because genetic results may take weeks to become available, the diagnosis and initial management of aHUS must not be delayed , but instead rely on clinical presentation and excluding other thrombotic microangiopathies. Prompt initiation of complement inhibitors, such as ravulizumab, is essential for achieving complete TMA response and hematologic normalization in the majority of patients, as demonstrated in pivotal trials . Once results are available, they provide valuable insights for tailoring the management plan, but should not delay life-saving therapy. —-------------
