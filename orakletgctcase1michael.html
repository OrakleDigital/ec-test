Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated diffuse synovial thickening, hemosiderin deposition, and multiple intra-articular nodules, consistent with diffuse TGCT. Given the extent of synovial involvement and functional limitations, treatment options are being discussed.

Case Review

Michael T. is a 34-year-old male diagnosed with diffuse TGCT of the right knee, confirmed by core needle biopsy and IHC. His biopsy revealed synovial proliferation with hemosiderin-laden macrophages, multinucleated giant cells, and mononuclear stromal cells, while IHC was positive for CSF1 and CD68, confirming the tumor’s macrophage-lineage origin and CSF1-driven pathogenesis.

Imaging studies─including MRI, CT, and MRA─demonstrated extensive synovial thickening, intra-articular nodular masses, joint effusion, and vascular invasion, with encasement and partial infiltration of adjacent arterial structures. While no osseous erosion or distant metastases were detected, the presence of vascular involvement significantly increases the risk of surgical complications, such as vascular injury, hemorrhage, and incomplete tumor resection.

Given his symptomatic disease with progressive pain, joint stiffness, mechanical symptoms, and confirmed vascular invasion, surgical resection alone poses an exceptionally high risk of morbidity and recurrence. As a result, systemic therapy is the preferred approach to mitigate further vascular compromise, reduce tumor burden, and preserve joint function.

CSF1R inhibitors have demonstrated efficacy in alleviating symptoms and improving quality of life (QoL) in these patients, yet uncertainty in treatment selection has led to inconsistent adoption of new therapies, often resulting in suboptimal patient outcomes. Hence, this case highlights the importance of a multidisciplinary approach, incorporating advanced imaging, vascular assessment, and evidence-based systemic therapy selection to optimize disease control while minimizing surgical risks and preserving joint function.

TGCT is a rare condition with varying incidence rates depending on subtype and location. Localized TGCT (nodular-type TGCT [N-TGCT]) is more common, with an incidence of 30–34 per million in digits and 11 per million in extremities, while D-TGCT occurs less frequently, at 5–8.4 per million [Mastboom MJL, et al, 2017]. Studies show a higher proportion of D-TGCT cases in specialized centers, likely due to the complexity of these cases. TGCT predominantly affects women and is typically diagnosed between ages 35 and 50. N-TGCT most commonly affects the hand and wrist, whereas D-TGCT primarily involves the knee, followed by the ankle and hip. Recurrence rates differ significantly, with N-TGCT recurring in 9%–14% of cases compared to 23%–72% for D-TGCT, and outcomes are worse when treatment is not performed at an expert center. Metastatic TGCT is extremely rare (<1 per million per year), with a high mortality rate of ~30% and a metastatic risk of 50%, making it primarily case-reportable [Stacchiotti S, et al, 2023; Mastboom MJL, et al, 2020].

N-TGCT versus D-TGCT

TGCT is divided into N-TGCT and D-TGCT, representing distinct clinical and pathological entities. N-TGCT, previously termed localized TGCT, is typically a single, slow-growing lesion, which arises in soft tissue near tendons or interphalangeal joints, with occasional bone erosion or skin involvement. It rarely affects large joints. D-TGCT, on the other hand, is characterized by widespread synovial infiltration, often extending into extra-articular structures, leading to hemarthrosis, bone and cartilage destruction, and a high recurrence rate. While TGCT causes significant morbidity, it is not fatal, except in the rare case of metastatic TGCT, which may arise spontaneously or after multiple recurrences of conventional TGCT [Stacchiotti S, et al, 2023; Righi A, et al, 2015].

The radiological differential diagnoses of n-TGCT in the hands and feet include fibroma of the tendon sheath, ganglion cysts, hemangiomas, angiomyomas, bizarre parosteal osteochondromatous proliferation, and tophaceous gout. These can be distinguished based on imaging characteristics such as mineralization patterns, T2-weighted signal intensity, and hemosiderin deposition. Intra-articular N-TGCT may also be confused with synovial chondroma or, in rare cases, synovial sarcoma, requiring biopsy for confirmation. For D-TGCT, the main differentials include synovial osteochondromatosis, rheumatic diseases, chronic non-specific synovitis, and hemophilic arthropathy, which can be differentiated based on the presence of mineralized loose bodies, bone marrow edema, and hemosiderin deposition on MRI [Stacchiotti S, et al, 2023].

Pathologically, fibroma of the tendon sheath may resemble N-TGCT but lacks mononuclear histiocytoid and epithelioid cells. Epithelioid sarcoma, papillary synovitis, and alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma can also mimic TGCT but are distinguishable via IHC . Angiomatoid fibrous histiocytoma may present similarly to D-TGCT but has syncytial histiocytoid cells and blood-filled alveolar spaces. In rare cases, intraosseous TGCT recurrence can resemble giant cell tumor of bone (GCTB) but lacks the H3.3 G34 mutation seen in GCTB. Accurate diagnosis requires a combination of imaging, histopathology, and immunohistochemical markers to distinguish TGCT from these mimickers [Stacchiotti S, et al, 2023].

Principles of Treatment

Active Surveillance vs Active Treatment in TGCT

The decision between active surveillance (AS) and active treatment in TGCT depends on symptom severity, tumor extent, and the potential risks of intervention. While asymptomatic patients are typically managed with AS, those with pain, joint instability, or functional impairment require treatment to prevent disease progression and joint damage. The choice between surgery or systemic therapy should be made within a multidisciplinary tumor board, considering the risk of recurrence and treatment-associated morbidity [Stacchiotti S, et al, 2023; Gronchi A, et al, 2021].

Surgical Considerations

Surgery is the preferred treatment for N-TGCT and D-TGCT when complete resection is feasible. N-TGCT can often be fully excised with low recurrence rates, while D-TGCT requires extensive synovectomy, which carries a higher risk of local recurrence and postoperative complications. Surgical techniques vary by joint location, and arthroscopic vs open synovectomy is debated based on recurrence risk. Joint replacement or megaprosthesis reconstruction may be required in advanced cases, while amputation is rarely considered in extreme cases of severe functional impairment [Stacchiotti S, et al, 2023; Gronchi A, et al, 2021].

Non-Surgical Treatment Approaches

For unresectable or high-risk surgical cases, systemic therapy is an option. CSF1R inhibitors have shown efficacy in reducing tumor burden and symptoms.

Currently, 2 agents—pexidartinib and vimseltinib—are FDA-approved for the treatment of TGCT [Dania V, et al, 2024; FDA, 2024], with additional therapies under investigation. The treatment landscape is rapidly evolving, with emerging agents such as pimicotinib and intravenous CSF1R inhibitors being explored in Pigmented Villonodular Synovitis (PVNS). Radiotherapy and cryotherapy remain investigational, with limited data supporting their routine use. Conventional chemotherapy is not effective in TGCT, except for rare cases of metastatic TGCT. The optimal duration of systemic therapy remains undefined, and treatment decisions should be individualized based on response, side effects, and patient preference [Stacchiotti S, et al, 2023].

QoL, Symptoms Management, and Physiotherapy

D-TGCT significantly impacts QoL due to pain, swelling, stiffness, and joint destruction, with symptoms persisting in 50% of patients even after treatment. Surgical resection and long-term systemic therapy can lead to joint damage and treatment-related toxicity, affecting daily activities, work, and financial stability. Patients often experience psychosocial distress, as TGCT is frequently perceived as a benign disease despite its impact on function and QoL. Patient-reported outcomes, such as pain scales and PROMIS-PF questionnaires, are crucial in guiding treatment decisions [Kaasa S, et al, 2018]. Pain management should involve a multidisciplinary approach, including analgesics, anti-inflammatory drugs, and opioids when necessary, with careful monitoring for long-term side effects [Kaasa S, et al, 2018]. Palliative care referrals may be needed for patients with significant distress or disease burden. Future research should focus on optimal physical therapy strategies, symptom burden by tumor site, and the effects of different treatments on QoL [Stacchiotti S, et al, 2023].
