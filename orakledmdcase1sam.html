Introduction

A 12-Year-Old Boy with Tooth Pain, Fatigue, and Muscle Weakness

History of Present Illness

Welcome. Your patient today is Sam L.

Sam, a 12-year-old boy, reports pain from a decayed tooth in the lower right jaw, which worsens with chewing and occasionally radiates to the ear. His parents also report a history of recurrent falls, clumsiness, and progressive fatigue. Over the past two years, he has had increasing difficulty rising from the floor and climbing stairs, often using his hands to push himself upright. There is no history of myalgia, and no cranial nerve symptoms.

Family and Social History

The patient's family history is significant for a maternal uncle who died at a young age from Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD).

Physical Examination Notes

On general physical examination, the child has an obese appearance and difficulty standing, walking, rising from a sitting position, and climbing stairs. Findings include proximal muscle weakness, calf hypertrophy, hamstring contractures, and a positive Gower’s sign. There is no muscle wasting or fasciculations, muscle tone is normal, and cranial nerve examination is normal. Intraoral examination shows an anterior open bite, left posterior crossbite, enlarged tongue, crowding in the lower anterior, and a carious 46.

Case Review

The diagnosis of DMD requires differentiation from other conditions that can cause progressive muscle weakness and elevated CK levels.

Key differential diagnoses include:

- BMD: a milder form of dystrophinopathy characterized by later onset and slower symptom progression .

- LGMDs also present with proximal muscle weakness but are differentiated by specific genetic mutations and histopathological findings .

- SMA should be considered, particularly in younger patients, as it features muscle atrophy and weakness due to anterior horn cell degeneration but does not typically show markedly elevated CK levels .

Inflammatory myopathies such as polymyositis or dermatomyositis might present similarly but often involve systemic symptoms and distinctive skin findings (Sarwar et al., 2023; Qudsiya & Waseem, 2023).

Lastly, metabolic myopathies like mitochondrial disorders or glycogen storage diseases can mimic DMD, with symptoms often exacerbated by exercise or fasting. These conditions require specialized biochemical and genetic testing to confirm the diagnosis .

Careful clinical evaluation, including muscle biopsy, genetic testing, and EMG, is essential to distinguish DMD from these conditions and ensure appropriate management. In this case, serum markers such as CK, LDH, and ALT levels were markedly increased. Muscle biopsy and EMG also revealed positive results (Birnkrant et al., 2018; Yiu & Kornberg, 2015; Sinha et al., 2017).

Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is the most common muscle dystrophy worldwide, caused by mutations in the dystrophin (DMD) gene, and, as a result, the body is unable to synthesize the dystrophin protein required for muscle contraction. Every time the muscle contracts, muscle damage occurs, which is repaired, but with deficient protein, resulting in repaired muscle that is also damaged. This continuous succession of damage and repair, and eventually replacement of muscle with fibrofatty tissue, is responsible for the clinical signs of progressive muscle wasting and degeneration that is usually evident by 3–4 years of age . Recent epidemiological analyses confirm Duchenne muscular dystrophy as the most common inherited neuromuscular disorder of childhood, with a global birth prevalence of approximately 1 in 3,500–5,000 live male births .

DMD is caused by mutations in the DMD gene encoding a protein called dystrophin, which localizes to the cytoplasmic face of the sarcolemma of the skeletal muscle, forming one component of a large glycoprotein complex (dystrophin-associated glycoprotein complex). Dystrophin consists of an N-terminal actin-binding domain, 24 spectrin-like repeat units interspersed with 4 hinge regions, followed by a cysteine-rich domain and a C-terminal domain. The cysteine-rich domain binds to laminin-2 through alpha and beta-dystroglycan, and therefore acts as a mechanical link between actin in the cytoskeleton and the extracellular matrix. The DMD gene contains 79 exons but accounts for only 0.6% of the gene; the rest is made up of large introns. The large size of the DMD gene makes it susceptible to mutations, leading to loss of function of dystrophin, resulting in a prematurely truncated and unstable dystrophin protein. The majority of mutations are intragenic deletions, which account for 65%–72% of all DMD patients. In general, frameshifting variants are associated with absent or near-absent dystrophin and a Duchenne phenotype, whereas in-frame variants more often correlate with Becker muscular dystrophy . The precise mechanism of how dystrophin deficiency leads to degeneration of muscle fibers remains unclear. The absence of dystrophin at the plasma membrane leads to delocalization of dystrophin-associated proteins from the membrane, disruption of the cytoskeleton with resultant membrane instability, and increased susceptibility to mechanical stress. In addition, altered membrane permeability and abnormal calcium homeostasis are thought to play a role, with increased cytosolic calcium concentration leading to activation of proteases such as calpains (Opas & Michalak, 2001).

Affected boys clinically present with difficulty in running or getting up from the ground, frequent falls, or toe-walking. Patients have a waddling gait, calf enlargement, and lumbar lordosis that disappear when sitting. There is weakness of the proximal muscles of the lower limb and patients use their hands and arms to “walk” up their own body from a squatting position due to lack of hip and thigh muscle strength, suggestive of Gower's sign . In this case, the affected child clinically presented with signs of delayed motor development, difficulty in walking and climbing stairs, positive Gower's sign, and muscle weakness. Oral manifestations include wide dental arches, a large tongue, delayed eruption, an open bite, and retrognathic facial morphology. The development of malocclusion in these patients is linked to the involvement of the orofacial muscles by the disease, which was apparent in the present case . Although not diagnostic, recognition of early functional signs (eg, frequent falls, difficulty rising, Gower’s sign) supports timely CK testing and referral for confirmatory evaluation as emphasized across existing guideline-based approaches .

Diagnosis is confirmed by molecular genetic testing identifying a pathogenic DMD gene variant, with markedly elevated serum creatine kinase (CK) levels providing strong supportive evidence; muscle biopsy and EMG are adjunctive investigations used when genetic confirmation is unavailable or inconclusive . The increased permeability of the sarcolemma in DMD leads to leakage of intracellular proteins such as CK into the circulation, resulting in markedly elevated serum CK levels; other enzymes, including ALT, AST, aldolase, and LDH, may also be elevated due to muscle injury (Yiu & Kornberg, 2008). Current guideline-based diagnostic pathways prioritize clinical suspicion with serum CK as part of the initial diagnostic approach, followed by confirmatory testing (including molecular methods where available); EMG and muscle biopsy are generally used when the diagnosis remains uncertain or confirmatory testing is not available .
