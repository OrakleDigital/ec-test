Introduction

History of Present Illness

Welcome. Your patient today is Sarah M.

Ms. Sarah M. is a 35-year-old woman with a history of Class 3 severe obesity (BMI 44 kg/m²), type 2 diabetes mellitus (A1c 6.1% on metformin), polycystic ovarian syndrome, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, and bilateral knee osteoarthritis causing chronic pain, who presents for weight management evaluation. She has a past history of opioid dependence related to uncontrolled joint pain, now in remission. Pain control has included pregabalin and duloxetine, both associated with weight gain.

Her psychiatric history is notable for bipolar disorder. She initially trialed risperidone, which provided limited benefit, and later olanzapine, which improved mood symptoms but caused substantial weight gain and worsened metabolic risk. She is now maintained on quetiapine, which has stabilized her mood and improved sleep but continues to contribute to weight gain.

Sarah currently weighs 128 kg, her highest adult weight, compared with a previous low of 70 kg in her early twenties prior to psychiatric treatment. She reports irregular eating patterns, frequent snacking, and cravings for sweets in the evenings, often relying on processed and convenience foods. Although her activity is limited by knee pain, she manages short daily walks around her neighborhood for exercise. A prior sleep study was negative for obstructive sleep apnea, though she continues to experience insomnia and non-restorative sleep. She remains in psychiatric follow-up and engaged in behavioral therapy. Current psychosocial stressors include caring for her chronically ill parent.

Case Review

This case illustrates the complexity of managing severe obesity in the context of multiple comorbidities and psychiatric illness. Sarah’s trajectory reflects how psychotropic medications, chronic pain, and psychosocial stressors can converge to accelerate weight gain, metabolic dysfunction, and quality-of-life impairment. Despite prior attempts with lifestyle modification and medical therapy, her clinical profile highlights the limitations of traditional approaches when obesity is compounded by bipolar disorder, antipsychotic use, and osteoarthritis-related immobility.

Evidence-based guidelines strongly support an integrated, multidisciplinary strategy for such patients. This includes ongoing behavioral and nutritional interventions, physical therapy tailored to functional limitations, and the timely use of anti-obesity medications such as GLP-1 receptor agonists or dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonists . Tirzepatide may be particularly appropriate in Sarah’s case given its superior weight loss efficacy compared with semaglutide and its proven benefits in patients with obesity complicated by type 2 diabetes . In addition, bariatric/metabolic surgery remains the most effective and durable treatment, with robust evidence for remission of type 2 diabetes, improvement in non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, and reduction in long-term cardiovascular risk .

Given Sarah’s BMI of 44 kg/m², type 2 diabetes, NAFLD, and osteoarthritis, she meets multiple Class I indications for surgery under ASMBS/IFSO 2022 guidelines .

Sarah’s case underscores the importance of personalized, stepwise obesity care that addresses psychiatric stability, metabolic health, and functional capacity in parallel. Clinicians must balance psychiatric medication needs with cardiometabolic risk, using weight-neutral or weight-lowering alternatives where feasible . Although quetiapine carries metabolic risk, it remains more favorable than olanzapine for weight considerations, reinforcing the importance of close coordination between psychiatry and obesity medicine . Ultimately, optimizing outcomes requires both guideline-directed pharmacologic and surgical interventions and a long-term commitment to behavioral support. Her elevated hs-CRP and HOMA-IR further highlight increased cardiometabolic risk, strengthening the rationale for aggressive intervention . This approach not only offers the greatest likelihood of sustained weight reduction but also improves physical health, psychological well-being, and overall quality of life .

The management of severe obesity has evolved significantly in recent decades, reflecting advances in pharmacotherapy, behavioral science, and metabolic surgery. Obesity is now recognized as a chronic, relapsing, multifactorial disease with biological, psychological, and environmental determinants, requiring treatment approaches beyond lifestyle modification alone .

Pharmacologic Therapy: Clinical guidelines from the Endocrine Society and the American Gastroenterological Association emphasize the role of anti-obesity medications (AOMs) as adjuncts to lifestyle interventions for patients with BMI ≥30 kg/m² or ≥27 kg/m² with comorbidities .

Among these, GLP-1 receptor agonists such as semaglutide and liraglutide, and more recently the dual GIP/GLP-1 agonist tirzepatide, have demonstrated superior efficacy in producing clinically meaningful weight loss and improving glycemic control . Head-to-head data suggest tirzepatide produces greater weight loss than semaglutide in patients with type 2 diabetes, making it an especially compelling option in patients with both obesity and diabetes .

Trials consistently show weight reductions of 15-20% with semaglutide and tirzepatide, accompanied by improvements in cardiometabolic outcomes, fatty liver disease, and quality of life . While phentermine/topiramate ER remains a guideline-supported alternative with high efficacy, its psychiatric and cognitive side effects limit use in vulnerable populations .

Other agents such as naltrexone/bupropion and orlistat provide modest benefit but are constrained by tolerability and safety concerns .

Behavioral and Lifestyle Interventions: Despite pharmacologic progress, behavioral modification remains the foundation of obesity care. Structured nutritional counseling, intensive behavioral therapy, and cognitive-behavioral strategies are strongly recommended to enhance adherence and sustain long-term weight reduction .

Physical activity interventions not only contribute to weight maintenance but also improve metabolic health, mood, and functional mobility—particularly relevant for patients with obesity complicated by musculoskeletal limitations such as osteoarthritis .

Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery: Surgical intervention continues to represent the most effective and durable treatment for severe obesity. The 2022 ASMBS/IFSO guideline recommends metabolic and bariatric surgery for individuals with BMI ≥ 35 kg/m² regardless of comorbidities, and to consider surgery for selected patients with BMI 30-34.9 kg/m² and inadequately controlled metabolic disease .

Long-term studies consistently demonstrate significant and sustained weight loss, remission of type 2 diabetes, improvement in dyslipidemia and NAFLD, and reductions in all-cause mortality . Importantly, surgery also confers psychiatric benefits, including improved quality of life and reduced depressive symptoms, when integrated within multidisciplinary care .

Integration of Psychiatric Considerations: Patients with psychiatric comorbidities present a unique therapeutic challenge. Antipsychotic medications, particularly second-generation agents, are strongly associated with weight gain and metabolic disturbances. Guidelines emphasize the need for close collaboration between psychiatry and obesity medicine, prioritizing weight-neutral agents when possible, and ensuring that behavioral and pharmacologic interventions for obesity are compatible with psychiatric stability. Quetiapine, though associated with weight gain, is metabolically less adverse than olanzapine, making careful selection and monitoring essential .

Summary: The literature supports a comprehensive, stepwise model of obesity management that combines behavioral, pharmacologic, and surgical approaches, tailored to individual risk profiles and comorbidities. Emerging evidence also highlights the role of inflammatory and insulin resistance markers, such as hs-CRP and HOMA-IR, as predictors of cardiometabolic risk and potential targets of intervention . This integrated framework is especially relevant in cases such as Sarah’s, where severe obesity intersects with diabetes, fatty liver disease, osteoarthritis, and bipolar disorder, demanding a balanced strategy to achieve durable weight reduction and improved health outcomes.
