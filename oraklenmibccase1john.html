Introduction

A 72-Year-Old Male with High-Grade Urothelial Carcinoma

History of Present Illness

Welcome. Your patient today is John D.

A 72-year-old male presented with painless hematuria and bladder discomfort, leading to a diagnosis of high-grade urothelial carcinoma (pT1; WHO 2004/2016 high-grade, corresponding to WHO 1973 grade 2–3) with papillary features and carcinoma in situ (CIS). He underwent transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT) followed by intravesical BCG immunotherapy. After the seventh BCG instillation, he developed severe lower urinary tract symptoms, including burning and painful urination, which worsened after the eighth instillation. Ten days after the ninth instillation, he experienced lower extremity swelling, prompting discontinuation of BCG therapy. Six months later, he developed severe sepsis with high fever, hypotension, and pronounced hematuria, requiring antibiotic treatment. Persistent urinary urgency and discomfort led to further cystoscopy, TURBT, and biopsy, which ruled out tuberculous cystitis but confirmed persistent high-grade urothelial carcinoma with CIS. Treatment with solifenacin and mirabegron improved urinary symptoms. Given the presence of BCG-unresponsive CIS and papillary NMIBC, further bladder-preserving treatment options were considered.

The patient also experienced lower limb swelling and weakness, suspected to be a systemic reaction to BCG therapy. Prednisolone (5 mg/day) provided significant improvement. Long-term follow-up revealed intermittent recurrence of urothelial carcinoma, with recent findings indicating high-grade disease (Paris System category IV).

Family and Social History

He is a former smoker, having smoked 1 pack per day for 30 years before quitting 15 years ago, and currently consumes alcohol occasionally (1–2 drinks per week). He leads a sedentary lifestyle due to his age and recent health issues. John lives with his wife, who assists with his care, and has 2 adult children living nearby who provide occasional support.

Physical Examination Notes

On physical examination, John appeared mildly pale. Genitourinary examination revealed bladder discomfort on palpation, and hematuria was noted on urinalysis. Bilateral pitting edema was observed in the lower extremities, particularly in the lower legs, which correlated with his complaints of swelling and weakness. No other significant abnormalities were detected during the examination.

Case Review

John’s case highlights the complexities of managing high-grade non–muscle invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) in an elderly patient, particularly in the context of treatment-related complications, BCG intolerance, and recurrent disease. The therapeutic strategies employed, including transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT), intravesical Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) immunotherapy, and subsequent management of infectious, inflammatory, and functional complications, were tailored to his clinical presentation, tumor characteristics, and overall health status in accordance with contemporary NMIBC management principles .

While multiple treatment options are valid for such cases, including Pembrolizumab and Nadofaragene Firadenovec for BCG-unresponsive disease and ureteral stent placement for upper tract involvement, this case focused on the challenges of balancing therapeutic efficacy with quality of life.

This case underscores the importance of individualized, risk-adapted treatment planning, close monitoring for therapy-related complications, and the integration of emerging bladder-preserving therapies to optimize outcomes for patients with high-risk NMIBC who are not ideal candidates for immediate radical cystectomy .

Introduction

NMIBC accounts for approximately 75% of newly diagnosed bladder cancer cases, and it is characterized by high recurrence rates and a variable but generally lower risk of progression to muscle-invasive disease. NMIBC is staged as Ta, T1, or carcinoma in situ (CIS), and its clinical management focuses on balancing tumor control with the risk of progression. Understanding the current landscape of NMIBC is essential for improving patient outcomes .

Recent evidence underscores the biological and clinical heterogeneity of NMIBC, where traditional staging alone may not fully capture prognosis. The integration of validated clinical risk models with emerging molecular and biomarker-based insights has been proposed to refine diagnosis and management .

Epidemiology and Risk Factors

Globally, bladder cancer is the tenth most common malignancy worldwide, with NMIBC contributing significantly to its prevalence. Smoking and occupational exposure to carcinogens such as aromatic amines are the strongest risk factors. Differences in incidence are also observed based on gender, ethnicity, and geographic region, with higher rates among males and older adults. These epidemiological patterns guide prevention and screening strategies .

Gontero et al highlight that the growing burden of bladder cancer in aging populations requires clinicians to account for comorbidity, frailty, and competing risks. Smani et al further emphasize that lifestyle and environmental exposures may influence recurrence risk and treatment tolerance.

Pathophysiology and Diagnosis

NMIBC tumors are graded and staged using WHO/ISUP criteria, with CIS and papillary tumors as common presentations. Diagnostic evaluation relies on cystoscopy, the gold standard for detecting bladder tumors. Advances in urinary biomarkers, such as CXCL10, complement traditional methods by providing non-invasive options for monitoring inflammation and tumor progression. These developments enhance the precision of NMIBC diagnosis .

Enhanced cystoscopy (blue light or NBI) improves detection of CIS and reduces early recurrence compared with white light cystoscopy . Cross-sectional imaging with CT urography is recommended in patients with hematuria or high-risk features to exclude synchronous upper tract urothelial carcinoma . Repeat TURBT within six weeks is recommended in T1 high-grade disease to confirm stage and ensure complete resection . Emerging diagnostic adjuncts, including urinary biomarkers and AI-based pathology, are under investigation but remain complementary to cystoscopy .

Initial Treatment and Risk Stratification

TURBT is the primary treatment for NMIBC, ensuring accurate staging and removal of visible tumors. Following TURBT, patients are stratified into risk groups to guide intravesical therapy decisions. Intravesical BCG remains the standard of care for high-risk NMIBC, but its limited availability and resistance issues have prompted the exploration of alternative therapies .

Both Smani et al., (2025) and Gontero et al., (2025) stress the importance of defining “BCG-unresponsive” disease, as this designation determines eligibility for bladder-sparing options such as pembrolizumab or nadofaragene firadenovec, or referral for early radical cystectomy in the highest-risk cases. Novel risk stratification models incorporating clinical, pathological, and molecular features are being developed to better guide therapy intensity .

Emerging Therapeutics

Innovations in NMIBC treatment focus on immune-based and novel intravesical therapies. Immune checkpoint inhibitors and next-generation intravesical agents, including mitomycin C formulations, are being evaluated for high-risk and BCG-refractory cases. These therapies aim to reduce recurrence rates and improve patient outcomes, especially in resistant NMIBC .

Surveillance and Follow-Up

Given NMIBC's high recurrence rates, risk-adapted surveillance is essential. Current guidelines recommend frequent cystoscopic surveillance (every 3 months initially), with intervals extended based on recurrence risk. Urinary biomarkers and advanced imaging techniques show promise in reducing the burden of invasive monitoring while maintaining diagnostic accuracy .

Enhanced cystoscopy during follow-up reduces recurrence risk through improved detection . Urinary biomarkers may help refine risk-adapted surveillance protocols, but they do not replace cystoscopy .

Outcomes and Prognosis

The prognosis of NMIBC depends on factors such as tumor grade, stage, and adherence to therapy. High-grade tumors have a higher risk of progression, underscoring the importance of personalized management. Long-term monitoring and early detection of recurrence are essential for optimizing outcomes .

Incorporating molecular classifiers into validated risk models may enhance the prediction of recurrence and progression, enabling more individualized care .

Future Directions

Integrating molecular diagnostics, biomarkers, and precision therapeutics into routine NMIBC management offers significant potential for improving care. Future research should focus on validating these innovations through large-scale trials and addressing disparities in access to treatment globally .
