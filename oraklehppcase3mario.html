Introduction

A 4-Year-Old Male with Musculoskeletal Pain, Delayed Motor Milestones and Premature Tooth Loss

History of Present Illness

Welcome. Your patient today is Mario M.

The patient is a 4-year-old male who has diffuse lower extremity musculoskeletal pain requiring regular painkillers. He also finds it difficult to walk for more than 15 minutes without getting tired, and struggles to participate in outdoor play due to pain. His parents report concerns about his ability to keep up with peers, describing his movements as slower and less coordinated than other children his age. He began walking later than expected, with ongoing delays in achieving motor milestones. Over the past year, the family has also observed the development of a waddling gait.

Approximately 4 months ago, he experienced premature loss of 2 primary teeth, initially thought to be related to minor trauma but concerning given his young age.

The pediatrician diagnosed rickets using imaging findings but ruled out vitamin D deficiency after normal laboratory results. Noticing the combination of premature tooth loss and skeletal abnormalities, the pediatrician referred the child to a pediatric endocrinologist.

Currently, the child’s physical limitations impact his ability to participate in age-appropriate activities, causing frustration and difficulty engaging socially with peers. The family is seeking a definitive diagnosis and further insights into the condition's progression and potential implications.

Family and Social History

Mario’s family history reveals a pattern of musculoskeletal and metabolic conditions. His father also has a history of dental issues. No other hereditary or chronic conditions have been reported in the family.

Mario lives with his parents and has an older sibling who is healthy and active. His parents report that Mario enjoys playing with peers but struggles to keep up due to physical limitations. He attends preschool, where teachers have noted difficulty with running and balance during playtime. The family does not own pets. His parents do not smoke, consume alcohol occasionally, and deny any use of illicit drugs. Mario’s diet is reportedly balanced, and the family provides adequate nutrition, including calcium and vitamin D intake.

This family and social history aligns with Mario’s clinical presentation and highlights relevant hereditary patterns and lifestyle factors.

Case Review

This case underscores the importance of recognizing key diagnostic clues and the challenges of diagnosing complex conditions in pediatric patients. Mario’s clinical history presented several early signs that warranted further investigation but were initially misinterpreted or overlooked. These included delayed motor milestones, diffuse musculoskeletal pain, muscle weakness, and premature loss of primary teeth without root resorption—hallmark features of HPP . A persistently low ALP level, a critical biochemical abnormality, was present early but was not initially linked to his symptoms due to the laboratory's application of an adult reference range.

Radiographic findings of femoral metaphyseal radiolucent areas, cupping, and fraying, consistent with rickets-like changes, combined with normal vitamin D, calcium, and phosphorus levels, should have prompted earlier consideration of metabolic bone disorders, such as hypophosphatasia, rather than an incorrect diagnosis of JIA.

Another missed clue was the lack of significant relief seen with nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, corticosteroids, and physical therapy, further delaying the search for the true underlying cause. The premature loss of primary teeth, initially attributed to trauma, represented another key systemic clue that was not fully explored, delaying the connection to a disorder affecting bone and dental mineralization.

Genetic testing was ordered to evaluate the underlying cause of his symptoms further. The test returned a positive, c.1403C>T (p.Thr468Met) variant, confirming a mutation in the ALPL gene. This supports the diagnosis but is not essential for confirmation, as his clinical and biochemical presentation already provides strong evidence.

Patients with HPP often endure a prolonged and challenging diagnostic journey, consulting multiple specialists before receiving an accurate diagnosis. HPP is a rare metabolic bone disorder caused by mutations in the ALPL gene, which result in deficient ALP activity . Its broad and variable clinical spectrum frequently leads patients to seek care from different specialists based on their presenting symptoms.

Orthopedic specialists are commonly consulted due to issues like bone pain, fractures, or delayed fracture healing, while rheumatologists may evaluate patients misdiagnosed with arthritis or joint pain. Dentists often encounter HPP in cases of premature tooth loss or abnormal dentin mineralization, a hallmark feature of the disease. Endocrinologists may become involved when bone metabolism abnormalities are suspected, and neurologists may evaluate severe cases presenting with seizures, including pyridoxine-responsive seizures .

HPP diagnosis relies on persistently low ALP levels, adjusted for age and sex, and elevated substrates like PLP and PPi . Imaging can reveal characteristic findings like osteopenia and fractures, and genetic testing of the ALPL gene can confirm the diagnosis, though it may not detect all cases .

Clinical evaluation is crucial in diagnosing HPP, particularly as symptoms often overlap with more common conditions such as rickets, rheumatoid arthritis, or fibromyalgia, leading to underdiagnosis or misdiagnosis. A strong clinical suspicion is essential, especially in patients with musculoskeletal pain, fractures, or dental abnormalities. In milder cases, symptoms may not fully manifest until adulthood despite persistently low ALP levels during childhood. While not always necessary for diagnosis , genetic studies are valuable for confirming hereditary patterns, understanding recurrence risks, and enabling prenatal diagnosis in affected families .

Differential Diagnosis of HPP

HPP shares many clinical and radiological features with other conditions, challenging its diagnosis and requiring careful differentiation. Common differential diagnoses include rickets and osteogenesis imperfecta, which may present with skeletal abnormalities, fractures, and growth issues .

In prenatal forms, osteogenesis imperfecta type II and achondrogenesis must be considered, as they can also show skeletal hypomineralization and narrow thorax on imaging. The infantile form of HPP may mimic conditions such as severe vitamin D deficiency, while the childhood form can resemble chronic recurrent multifocal osteomyelitis, particularly when bone marrow edema is visible on MRI (Choida & Bubbear, 2019).

In adults, HPP can be confused with osteoporosis, especially in recurrent fractures and low bone density cases. These patients may also show atypical femoral pseudofractures originating from the subtrochanteric region, necessitating differentiation from other forms of osteomalacia, such as X-linked hypophosphatemia, where fractures occur in different anatomical locations, like the medial femoral cortex. Additionally, calcium pyrophosphate dihydrate crystal deposition disease (CPPD) and its manifestations, such as pseudogout and calcific periarthritis, may overlap with HPP due to the accumulation of extracellular pyrophosphate. (Choida & Bubbear, 2019).

Accurate diagnosis requires evaluating low serum alkaline phosphatase (ALP) levels, elevated pyridoxal phosphate (PLP), and other biochemical markers while carefully ruling out these differential diagnoses through clinical, radiological, and genetic evaluations.

The Essential Components of a Medical/Dental History Questionnaire for Suspected HPP Patients

A medical and dental history questionnaire for suspected HPP should thoroughly assess symptoms and risk factors to aid in diagnosis. It should include pain assessment tools like the Brief Pain Inventory short form to evaluate pain severity and its impact on daily life, and the Health Assessment Questionnaire Disability Index to measure difficulties in performing daily activities such as walking, eating, and dressing. Quality of life should also be assessed using instruments like the 36-Item Short-Form Health Survey, focusing on physical and mental health impacts .

Diagnosing Hypophosphatasia (HPP) requires a thorough assessment of medical and dental history to identify its hallmark clinical features. According to existing studies, skeletal abnormalities are among the most frequently reported signs, particularly in pediatric patients . Delayed motor milestones, impaired mobility, and difficulty gaining weight or short stature further contribute to the diagnostic picture, especially in younger populations.

Dental history is also important, with premature tooth loss frequently cited as an early indicator. Biochemical markers, particularly persistently low alkaline phosphatase (ALP) levels adjusted for age and sex, and radiological evidence of osteopenia or other skeletal defects, serve as essential diagnostic tools. Together, these elements provide a robust foundation for identifying HPP and distinguishing it from other conditions with overlapping symptoms, such as rickets or fibromyalgia.

In addition to clinical and biochemical assessments, understanding the broader impact of HPP on patients' quality of life is essential. Mobility challenges and limitations in performing daily activities reflect the systemic burden of the disease .

Capturing these aspects in a medical and dental history ensures a comprehensive understanding of the disease's impact on physical and functional well-being.

Age and Sex-Adjusted ALP Levels

Age- and sex-adjusted ALP levels are a cornerstone in the diagnosis of HPP, as persistently low ALP levels are a hallmark feature of the disorder. Accurate interpretation of ALP measurements requires careful consideration of physiological variations across different age groups and sexes.

Moreover, the CALIPER study establishes age- and sex-specific reference intervals for key biochemical markers, including alkaline phosphatase (ALP), crucial for diagnosing metabolic bone disorders like hypophosphatasia (HPP). By using pediatric-specific data, it prevents misinterpretation caused by adult reference ranges, ensuring accurate clinical assessments .

Pediatric reference ranges for ALP are higher due to active bone growth and skeletal development, while adult levels are generally lower and stabilize with age . Failing to account for these variations can lead to misdiagnosis or overlooked cases.

ALP levels should be evaluated alongside other biochemical markers to strengthen the diagnosis. Elevated levels of pyridoxal-5’-phosphate, the active form of vitamin B6, and increased urinary PEA are characteristic of HPP and support the findings of low ALP. Additionally, calcium and phosphate levels in HPP are typically normal or elevated , further distinguishing it from other conditions like rickets or osteomalacia, where these markers are usually abnormal.

While biochemical data are critical, confirmatory genetic testing for ALPL mutations can validate the diagnosis and provide insights into the inheritance pattern. The integration of age- and sex-adjusted ALP measurements with biochemical and genetic findings allows for a comprehensive and accurate approach to diagnosing HPP, ensuring timely and informed clinical decision-making .

Patients with a Personal or Family History of Dental Disease or Arthritis

The diagnosis of HPP is more likely in patients with a personal or family history of dental disease or arthritis, making these features key components of clinical evaluation. Dental indicators such as premature tooth loss, particularly during childhood with intact tooth roots, are hallmark signs of HPP. A family history of abnormal tooth development or frequent dental procedures further raises suspicion for the disorder .

Arthritic-like symptoms also provide important diagnostic clues. Chronic joint pain or stiffness without significant inflammation is a common feature of HPP, and a family history of osteoarthritis or early joint replacements may reflect underlying metabolic bone abnormalities consistent with the condition . Multigenerational patterns of recurrent fractures, bone deformities, or dental issues can further strengthen the likelihood of HPP, highlighting the genetic nature of the disease. These familial trends emphasize the importance of obtaining a detailed family and dental history.

By integrating this information into screening practices, clinicians can recognize HPP earlier, thereby facilitating timely diagnosis.
