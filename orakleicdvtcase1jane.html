Case Review

Venous thromboembolism occurs frequently in patients with cancer and is the second leading cause of death, after the cancer itself .

There is a well-established consensus of the need for anticoagulation in patients with a proximal deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism. But with improved imaging technology and widescale use of contrast imaging for cancer staging, many incidental pulmonary emboli are detected in patients with cancer. Furthermore, many isolated distal deep vein thromboses and subsegmental pulmonary emboli are identified. There have been questions if these small or asymptomatic thromboses require anticoagulation management similar to more proximal or symptomatic thromboses .

Recent evidence and international guidelines emphasize that, even for these small or incidental events, the risk of recurrence or progression in patients with active cancer remains clinically meaningful. The ESMO 2023 Clinical Practice Guideline and the NCCN 2025 guideline both recommend anticoagulation for incidental PE, isolated subsegmental PE, and isolated distal DVT in patients with cancer, provided there are no contraindications, reflecting the high thrombotic burden in this population . Her ECOG performance status of 1 supports full anticoagulation as she remains functionally independent with low bleeding risk from immobility . This guidance underscores the principle that cancer-associated thrombosis, regardless of location or symptomatic presentation, warrants therapeutic anticoagulation to mitigate morbidity and mortality.

The Association of Thrombosis and Cancer

The incidence of both arterial and venous thromboses is increased in cancer, compared with the general population . In this OGR, we will focus on cancer-associated venous thrombosis (CAT), comprising DVT and PE. In patients with cancer, CAT is a major cause of morbidity and mortality and the second leading cause of death after the cancer itself . The association of VTE with cancer has been recognized as early as 1865. CAT is arguably the earliest recognized paraneoplastic syndrome. Activation of the coagulation system by cancer not only leads to thrombosis but may also be related to a more aggressive cancer phenotype . Therefore, the association of VTE with cancer has been the subject of much interest, for both management and the possible relationship of the coagulation system and tumor biology. Despite great interest, anticoagulation intervention has not been shown to favorably affect cancer progression-free or overall survival in any prospective clinical trial in patients with cancer. The indication and application of anticoagulation in patients with cancer remain to treat and prevent the morbidity and potential mortality of VTE.

In most circumstances of a newly diagnosed VTE, the decision to treat is straightforward. In the absence of appropriate intervention, patients who have experienced a symptomatic PE are at high risk of death from the acute event and at high risk of an early recurrent event. In a seminal randomized study of patients with symptomatic PE in 1960, Barritt and Jordan (1960) showed that acute anticoagulation with a regimen of heparin followed by a vitamin K antagonist (VKA) significantly reduced thrombosis recurrence and thrombosis-related death compared with no anticoagulation control. Rightfully, no one has repeated that placebo-controlled study. Barritt and Jordan (1960) established the treatment paradigm of anticoagulation for an acute VTE, followed by longer-term anticoagulation for reduction in the risk of recurrent VTE.

With markedly improved diagnostic tools and commonplace use of routine contrast imaging in patients with cancer, many small thromboses are identified, including subsegmental PE and small distal (calf vein) DVT. As the risk of acute hemodynamic compromise with these small thromboses is considered low, do they warrant comparable anticoagulation as a larger, more proximal thrombosis? A similar question relates to the management of an incidental PE. An incidental PE is an event detected by staging contrast imaging and not because of clinically evident signs or symptoms. Recognizing the clinical burden and cost of anticoagulation, questions have been raised of whether all small or asymptomatic VTEs should be treated as one would treat a proximal or symptomatic VTE in patients or if they can be treated differently. This case is one of a minimally symptomatic distal (below the popliteal vein) DVT, with confirmation of no proximal vein involvement and no known PE (Soff, 2024b). Does the need to treat paradigm hold for a distal DVT in a patient with cancer?

Standard Approach of Anticoagulation Is Based on Two Periods of Risk

An acute DVT event is often associated with signs and symptoms of inflammation of the involved extremity and risk of embolization. With an acute PE, the patient may experience chest pain, respiratory compromise, and hemodynamic instability to the point of sudden death. A DVT may extend proximally, further increasing the risk of catastrophic PE and increasing the vascular compromise in the affected extremity. A PE may extend to larger vessels, increasing the risk of hemodynamic compromise and death. Contemporary anticoagulation therapy significantly reduces the risks of these adverse sequelae. Despite anticoagulation management, estimates are that 30-day mortality after a PE is approximately 10% . Much of the mortality risk of VTE is observed as sudden death, where the patient has a catastrophic initial presentation of PE with cardiorespiratory collapse, before an opportunity for intervention . Precise numbers are difficult to determine, but one recent review cited a 30% mortality rate in patients with untreated PE, reduced to 8% with timely therapy .

By 3 months after an acute VTE, the thrombus is usually organized with little risk of embolization. The need for anticoagulation beyond that period is determined by the risk of recurrent thrombosis. Studies that explored shorter courses of anticoagulation in patients with low-risk events have indicated that 6 weeks of anticoagulation is less effective than 12 weeks, with a higher rate of recurrence . For patients who have experienced a VTE in a highly provoked transient setting, that is, after major surgery, most patients do not need anticoagulation beyond 3–6 months. Anticoagulation beyond that timeframe is largely determined by the presence of other ongoing risk factors, such as persistent comorbidities, active cancer, or post-thrombotic syndrome .

Can We Identify VTE Events in Patients With Cancer Where the Risk of Acute Exacerbation or Recurrent VTE Is Sufficiently Low to Not Warrant Anticoagulation?

Incidental PE and Small Thromboses

There are three situations where the scope of the initial VTE may, at first, appear of sufficiently low clinical burden that the need for therapeutic anticoagulation may be questioned. These include incidental PE, isolated subsegmental PE, and isolated calf vein DVT. While the acute risk of morbidity and mortality from these events is lower than with a proximal VTE, the key question to guide anticoagulation is the risk of new or recurrent VTE.

With increasing imaging resolution and increasing use of routine contrast CT imaging, it is common to find a PE in a patient for whom the scan was performed for cancer staging rather than symptoms attributed to the thrombosis. These are referred to as incidental PE. den Exter et al. (2011) compared the risk of recurrent VTE in patients with cancer with an incidental or symptomatic PE. The 12-month cumulative incidence of recurrent VTE was similar in the two groups, and the standard of care has been established to treat them the same.

There has been a similar question of need to treat an isolated subsegmental PE. A multicenter prospective cohort study of patients with an isolated subsegmental PE evaluated if withholding anticoagulation may be acceptable. Patients were confirmed not to have a proximal DVT on baseline ultrasonography and on repeated ultrasonography between days 5 and 7. The study assessed low-risk patients as patients with cancer and pregnant patients were excluded. Patients were monitored without anticoagulation. The study was stopped prematurely when the predefined stopping rule was met, demonstrating a recurrent VTE rate of 3.1% over the 90-day follow-up. This event rate, higher than anticipated, indicated that a subsegmental PE still warrants anticoagulation, even in a relatively low-risk population. Half of the new or recurrent events were DVTs, consistent with the paradigm that the need for anticoagulation treatment of a VTE is informed in large measure by the risk of a new thrombosis since these new lower extremity DVTs were known not to be present at the time of initial subsegmental PE.

A similar study has not been performed in patients with cancer. But given that patients with cancer are at high risk of VTE, therapeutic anticoagulation for an isolated subsegmental PE is recommended, not simply to treat the initial thrombotic event, but because of the high risk of recurrence or new thrombosis.

The third circumstance to consider is an isolated calf deep vein thrombosis (ICDVT), defined as thrombosis confined to the infrapopliteal veins of the lower limbs. Does an ICDVT require anticoagulation? The conventional wisdom is that distal vein DVT is associated with a lower rate of pulmonary embolism than a proximal DVT, and therefore, the indication for anticoagulation is not as well-established as in patients who present with a proximal DVT or PE (Palareti & Schellong, 2012). In the noncancer patient population, 7%-10% of patients with a suspected PE have been shown to have an ICDVT, suggesting that the true risk of embolization from an ICDVT is clinically relevant.

Ageno et al. (2022) performed a prospective, randomized trial of ICDVT, comparing 6 weeks and 12 weeks of rivaroxaban therapy. Significantly more patients experienced a recurrent VTE in the 6-week treatment cohort, lending support to the paradigm of needing 3 months of anticoagulation for an acute DVT, even if small and distal. This was a low-risk population, excluding patients with cancer. Therefore, at least 3 months of anticoagulation is appropriate for an ICDVT in patients with cancer. No prospective clinical trials have adequately addressed this question in the noncancer population.

A number of small case series and cohort studies of patients with cancer-associated ICDVT have previously suggested that the risk of new or recurrent VTE is comparable to more proximal DVTs. This question was addressed in an analysis of the international Registro Informatizado de la Enfermedad TromboEmbolica venosa (RIETE) registry of patients with VTE by Galanaud et al. (2024). They focused on three cohorts: patients with cancer-associated distal DVT, cancer-associated proximal DVT, and non-cancer-associated distal DVT. There was no difference in the 90-day and 1-year rates of recurrent VTE in patients with cancer-associated proximal and ICDVT. Over 90% of patients in each group were treated with anticoagulants for at least 90 days. For patients with cancer and ICDVT, the odds ratio of recurrent thrombosis was twice as high as in patients without cancer with ICDVT.

The evidence to date is fairly clear. One cannot identify a subset of cancer-associated thromboses that is of sufficiently low risk for new or recurrent thrombosis to support a less-intensive treatment strategy. For incidental PE, isolated subsegmental PE, or an ICDVT, the risk of new or recurrent thrombosis is sufficiently high to warrant standard therapeutic anticoagulation.

Choice and Dose of Anticoagulant

As early as 1937, successful treatment of DVT and PE with heparin and vitamin K antagonists (VKA, i.e., warfarin) was reported as case series reviewed in Barritt and Jordan (Barritt & Jordan, 1960). As those were the only two anticoagulants available, there was little choice in which to use. Patients with cancer were treated the same as any other patient, although it was recognized that patients with cancer had a higher rate of bleeding and recurrent thrombosis than noncancer patients . This paradigm changed in 2003, when Lee et al. (2003) published the CLOT trial, demonstrating that low–molecular-weight heparin (LMWH), dalteparin, was significantly more effective than warfarin in patients with cancer. The hazard ratio of recurrent cancer-associated thrombosis was reduced by half with dalteparin. This led to a paradigm shift, first by demonstrating that thrombosis in cancer patients may require a different treatment strategy compared with the noncancer population, and more specifically that LMWH was clearly superior to VKA in this setting.

The next advance was the introduction of the direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs). DOACs are a class of orally available anticoagulants that do not require a cofactor or metabolic processing for activity. DOACs directly inhibit activated coagulation factors (most target Factor Xa), rather than reducing synthesis of functional factors, as is the case with warfarin and other vitamin K antagonists. Currently, there are five DOACs that have been FDA-approved for at least one indication. Only rivaroxaban and apixaban have been validated and approved as initial therapy for a VTE, without a lead-in period of a parenteral anticoagulant, and these are the two agents most widely used.

Several randomized clinical trials compared treatment of CAT with a DOAC with LMWH (reviewed in the studies by Frere et al. (2022) and Khorana et al. (2018)). DOACs significantly decreased the risk of recurrent thrombosis by one third, compared with LMWH . There was no significant increase in the risk of major bleeding, but a significant increase in the rate of clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding . This led to the widespread adaptation of the DOACs as the anticoagulant of choice for CAT as they are much easier for patients compared with the once- or twice-daily injection of LMWH. With the availability of DOACs for treatment of CAT, there is evidence that hemodynamically stable patients may safely initiate anticoagulation as outpatients, avoiding the need for hospitalization .

The bleeding risk with DOACs correlates with the presence of luminal gastrointestinal or genitourinary tract tumors or other pathologies . As this association has become more widely recognized, the safety profile of DOACs for treatment of CAT has improved. A recent guidance document from the International Society of Thrombosis and Haemostasis states, “We suggest the use of specific DOACs for cancer patients with an acute diagnosis of VTE, a low risk of bleeding, and no drug–drug interactions with current systemic therapy. LMWHs constitute an acceptable alternative.” Furthermore, “We suggest the use of LMWHs for cancer patients with an acute diagnosis of VTE and a high risk of bleeding, including patients with luminal gastrointestinal cancers with an intact primary, patients with cancers at risk of bleeding from the genitourinary tract, bladder, or nephrostomy tubes, or patients with active gastrointestinal mucosal abnormalities such as duodenal ulcers, gastritis, esophagitis, or colitis” .

While untreated CNS lesions are associated with increased risk of bleeding, standard practice does not call for CNS imaging before initiation of anticoagulation. There is no evidence to favor apixaban or rivaroxaban over the other, for both efficacy or safety. On the basis of the current medical literature, there is no guidance to use a different anticoagulant dose intensity for patients with cancer, compared with noncancer patients .

There are no data to guide the choice of a DOAC or LMWH or dosing in the setting of renal insufficiency (CrCl <30 ml/min) as those patients were excluded from the clinical trials of a DOAC versus LMWH . Apixaban, the DOAC with the least renal clearance, may be an appropriate choice in this setting, as would renal dose-adjusted enoxaparin.

Duration of Anticoagulation

The decision to continue anticoagulation beyond the standard 6 months for most VTE is based on an assessment of the risk of new or recurrent thrombosis. Persistent cancer and cancer-directed therapies are important ongoing major risks. If this patient has not achieved a complete remission and remains on cancer-directed therapy after 6 months of anticoagulation, the standard of care would be to continue anticoagulation .

There have been studies suggesting that a reduced dose of rivaroxaban (10 mg once daily) or apixaban (2.5 mg twice daily) may be warranted after the initial course of anticoagulation has been completed, when the indication for longer-term anticoagulation is not clear . Those studies were for lower-risk patients, excluding active cancer. Ongoing studies are looking to address this question .

Until evidence becomes available, patients with active cancer or undergoing cancer-related therapy should remain on full-dose anticoagulation, unless contraindications are identified.
